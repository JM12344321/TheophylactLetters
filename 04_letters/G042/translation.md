## English Translation

Not even once, my noble and good friend! If you were pursuing the philosophy of Pythagoras, you ought to cling to the ancestral rule of silence, and we would not be right to accuse you. But if you study the things of the Peripatos and boast in the inspired Aristotle, why do you restrain your tongue and disown your fathers?

Were not Theophrastus and Demetrios devotees of that leading teacher? The first received his name from speaking in a more divine manner; the second, the Phalerean, produced an important treatise On Interpretation of Speech. Yet you neglect us, though we once entertained and feasted you with rhetoric as best we could.

Perhaps in this you already think in Peripatetic fashion, if Aristotle's neglect of Plato is to be believed. But perhaps one should rather accept this: that the inspired Aristotle was displeased with the divine Plato's tendency toward ancient ways, and tears him apart in many places to preserve higher things and persuade us not to receive heavenly matters in a low and underhanded manner. If this is how you understand the difference between the two men, you still do not make ancestral your neglect of those who were once your teachers.

So you are caught by us, drawing after you many twisted chains of syllogisms. Yet behold, you escape me by cutting the knots with the sword of the office you bear. You will no doubt blame the disturbance of affairs, saying it does not pour confusion over the steadiness of your leisure for better things. To these excuses you will add your letters to us. You will also say a second thing: that you converse with us by pouring out your heart to the moderate and truly good John, so that we vainly accuse you, an innocent man.

This part of your defense I accept all the more. For that good John, writing to us, praised your kindness toward him in countless ways and seemed to deposit no ungrateful soul in his letters. Therefore shoot in this way, anointing your arrows with honey, and we, while demanding letters, will not indict you for lawlessness against friendship or speech. We receive the debts you pay to John.

It is clear that our paroikoi and, simply, all the affairs of the Church will pass bright days if they receive through him your sweetness borne along in a great stream. May God, the Father of wisdom, keep you above every evil and injury.

## Translator's Notes

- This is a playful reproach for silence, framed through Pythagorean silence, Aristotelian speech, and Theophrastus/Demetrios as models.
- Theophylact accepts indirect communication through "the good John" as a partial payment of friendship's debt.
