## Revised English Translation

"The man is no longer himself; he gives way again."

I had not yet set foot in Achrida, and already, lovesick wretch that I am, I longed for the city that has you in it. From far away a deadly vapor struck me, just such as the mouths of Charon breathe. For Empedocles, Strife produces heads without necks; but here the rule of incompetence has produced countless necks upon countless necks, all without heads. What Achridian is not a headless neck, knowing how to honor neither God nor man?

I have been condemned to live among such monstrosities. And the worst thing is that there is no hope that these necks will ever be furnished with heads by any stronger powers, as the friendship of the sage of Acragas brought incomplete heads toward completion. So the wings of my better hopes have wholly fallen away. The eagle on Zeus' scepter goes on foot, alas, is caught in the mud, and endures life with frogs.

They think it dreadful, and unworthy of the fortunes of their time, if they do not indulge themselves in every way upon the bird of Zeus. They mount upon his back, full of mud and stench; and once mounted, they cry out something ugly and out of tune. What else should be expected from a marshy soul? They seem, at any rate, to sing some sort of victory paean. But the eagle's beak has not yet been blunted, nor have the points of his talons. They can still tear and strip flesh. Yet he considers retaliation and vengeance for their outrage a greater outrage to himself; and so he looks only toward the rays of the sun, with the fixed and piercing gaze for which nature fitted both his eyes. He seems to make the sun, whoever that sun may be, a witness of his suffering, and by facing it to shame it, showing that such a creature's being mocked by such creatures is a cause for indignation. [The printed text is corrupt at this point.]

Then, by the pure rays of that sun, with which you yourself are illumined each day and with which you illumine the eagle-like young you beget, most philosophical and most gracious of men: does this insulted eagle long in vain to live with you among the blessed, where the sky lies wide and clear, after being dragged out of mud and mire? I do not think you will say so. Otherwise may you be condemned to this lake of evils here, and to the frogs that are its offspring. I have nothing else to curse you with that is more painful than your usual gout.

You may think I am jesting in this way; yes, by eloquence itself, because you are so full of graces to me, and because even remembering you so melts me that this very thing shows itself in my letters to you. At such moments I am released from every sorrow and borne freely toward your graces by other graces. But may God, the true giver of grace, grant me one day either complete release from the evils here or at least consolation; and may he grant you complete release from the pitiless beast, or at least respite. For surely either we are not the only ones who have strayed from his road, or he has not given only to us the unmixed cup of the fall.

## Source And Revision Notes

- The opening quotation is tagged by Gautier to Euripides, Phoenician Women 920.
- The headless-neck passage depends on Empedocles' cosmogonic imagery; Gautier also notes Homeric and biblical echoes later in the letter.
- Gautier marks the text as corrupt around the eagle's appeal to the sun; the translation is deliberately conservative there.
