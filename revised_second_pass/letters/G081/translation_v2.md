## Revised English Translation

From where, and with what thanks, could I repay you, my most magnificently distinguished son and lord in the Lord, since you show me every time to be the truest of all men? The praises with which I seem to ennoble you, you yourself ennoble still more by demanding them in return - not as sophistic flatteries of a rhetorician, but as leaps of the Spirit rejoicing in things truly noble, and as songs from a mouth that knows how to bless virtue.

For behold what was reported to me a little while ago: how highly truth-loving and unenvious ears would prize it. By a single trophy you lowered the brows of two nations, and with the tower of Persian madness you pulled down the height of Frankish madness as well. You appeared to us as a new Phinehas, piercing together with your mighty spear two nations unclean before the Lord.

The man who used to tax Nesman and the other Greek cities around the Pontus within the Tanais River and Lake Maeotis - including the chief city of the Colchians, bounded by the Phasis, and the city set high over Little Armenia, indeed all Armenia, to say nothing of the Mariandynians, Galatians, and neighboring Cappadocians - had grown fat and heavy from tax-levies. His tax-collecting hands have now been cut off by the edge of the sword, and he has learned instead to be taxed. His raids, with which he had become bold in plundering, were checked; he abandoned the ambushes that were more obvious to Taronites than a city set on a mountain. He recognized himself as dry-handed, or rather handless, for pitched battles; and cities of ten thousand men which he had captured with difficulty he lost more quickly than speech can tell. Thus the die of fortune has fallen for him into the heaviest misfortune. Rather, the scales of God tipped toward him the pan of your virtues, heavier than the deeds of those on whom he feasted before your command.

From this he has become convinced that the ancient trophies sung of the Romans were no myth - indeed, that what is said about them falls short of the truth. In you he sees the truth. I will even say that he thinks you surpass those men by as much as you, setting out from more deficient preparations, match yourself against them; so mighty is the impulse you give to what affairs lack. He wishes you had never been born, yet admires you as a noble man born. He curses your birth and calls those who begot you blessed. He prays childlessness for you, so that your line may be enclosed in you, but for himself he prays for the births of such children. And now, after being lifted so high by former good fortunes, he understands by experience that he was lifted so that he might suffer more when deprived of more. He has only one consolation: that he has suffered so much at the hands of the bravest of Romans, for the defeated also love to be glorified by the greatness of those who defeat them. If Homer were present, he would now have said of you also that a noble man fled, and a greater man pursued him.

Be hopeful too, fairest of cities, Neocaesarea, that before long you will have Gregory as your liberator. You are not unused to being benefitted by men named Gregory; and once you have scraped away the old age of your present misery, you will truly appear as New-Caesarea. You have pledges in your neighbors. Pray for the noble bridegroom who will be joined to you by victory over you and crowned with a crown of precious stone; for Christ sets up the trophies for Taronites.

The godless Turk now, because of you, bends downward and sees only what lies at his feet - he who yesterday swept the arrogant pupils of his eyes everywhere and planned to wipe out land and sea together. Therefore he now looks to the terms of agreement as his only helpers of salvation. He stretches out his hands no longer to draw the sword, but to give pledges; laying aside the bow, he takes up the herald's staff, and he desires to have as a firm friend the man whom he found unconquerable as an enemy.

The Frank, whose neck was iron until now but proved softer than warm wax, bows to you in his abundance, and through you to our mighty emperor. And why should he not, when he sees the one he himself had worshipped now worshipping, and the one who bound others held in bonds more unbreakable by the will of Taronites? He is so mastered by these bonds that he grants to the emperors his own ransom, though he once pursued it and counted it an affair worth everything to possess as a purchased slave the man who made himself liberator of all the East. He wandered in such long dreams that he would not tolerate even second place in imperial honor; but you alone have prospered as the agent of the thing pursued. For the Turk, compelled by your hands, unwillingly agreed to truces and to other terms, including the surrender of the Frank to our unconquered emperor.

For these things I owe you thanks in another way as well. As has been said, it is not only that I am shown truthful by you, but also that those who bear the name of Christ in common, and the Roman rule, receive good counsel and the help of prayer. As for our all-good emperor, what need is there to say how worthily he will repay your wise counsel and courage? Always preferring you before all - for what emperor has ever been more skilled than he in judging men? - he gives you to affairs most in need of active oversight, like a most skilled physician set against terrible diseases and a helmsman set against a storm.

But what a thing a letter is! It is truly a bridle holding back a rhetorical mouth and refusing to allow it to revel at greater length in noble deeds. By it I too am reined back from eagerness to say more. If free speech were available to me, I would run verbal races for you, not falling short of your chariot of virtues so far as words can say - for that chariot is driven for you with wings. But since what comes from me is sufficient, and perhaps not worthy to be hissed at in the theater of speech, I shall say only this and then stop.

Lift your eyes to the mountains, from where your help has come, and will come again and again. Do not cast them downward; do not turn them toward yourself. Things so great do not belong to human strength, either alone or chiefly, but wholly - or at least mostly - to divine grace. In God you do power, the God who crushes enemies. You will confirm this grace in yourself, and draw still more of it, by being grateful to the one who grants grace. You would become the image of him if you are conformed to him through goodness, poured out upon rich and poor, upon those who are sacred in the stricter sense and those who are commoner - or, to speak better, upon the more divine and the more human - insofar as each has need of it. Through this goodness may you be delivered from evils and from the Evil One, so that you neither suffer harm nor become corrupted.
