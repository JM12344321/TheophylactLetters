## Revised English Translation

My holy lord: [the opening is damaged in the PG/OCR], and you seem to have forgotten my lowliness, or at least not even to have learned how things stand with me. Truly, those who once made war on all heaven piled Ossa upon Pelion, or, if you like, the giants set a crown upon the tower of Babel. If, however, you do keep us in memory and look about for news of our condition, lend me your ears for a little while: learn a few things, and infer the rest from what is left unsaid.

The myths make Heracles, glorious by Zeus and by the Fates, the slave of Omphale. Omphale was queen of the Lydians, a woman soft, rich in gold, wishing to seem most beautiful, insulted if anyone compared her with a rose, disdaining even the arms of Hera, the hair of Leto, the ankles of Hebe, and the feet of Thetis, wishing in every respect to be one image of Aphrodite.

While the victorious hero served her, he sat among the Lydian women and carded purple wool. Sometimes he stood near the storeroom and scattered the wool-baskets. At other times he handed around the cup, gripping it with great and noble fingers that trembled because they were so unpracticed in wine-serving. For this he was struck with a golden ring, and, handed over to a Lydian eunuch slave as tutor, he had trouble learning the cupbearer's art. His fingers longed for the club. The eunuch even plucked the beard of his pupil; being a eunuch, he was drawn toward hair. There grief helped Heracles in accomplishing the task. It wasted away his fingers, and carrying the cup became possible for thinner ones.

Nor was this all for the hero born after three nights. He also shared the work of the women grinding grain. He hummed along with the female millers; and when they tuned up something shrill and broken, he answered in a rough, straight tone. They burst out laughing, clapped their hands, left the work, and ordered him alone to turn the mill like an unrhythmic donkey. The son of Zeus suffered these things and endured that outrage until the appointed times released him from slavery. Then he labored again, but with deeds worthy of Zeus and of his journeying, for he purified the world of its disorders.

I have written you my own portrait in the condition where I now am. The difference is only this: I am not serving a rich, clean, beautiful queen, altogether a golden Aphrodite, but barbarian slaves, unclean men reeking of sheepskins, poorer in life than they are rich in malice, or rather kings at once in poverty of life and in malice altogether.

Release me from this shameful slavery, if you can; otherwise I shall die early, for Christ's sake, a son who has done nothing worthy of his father. That will be inglorious for me, and perhaps no less shameful for those who tolerate it.

So I set foot again in Achrida, and in my lovesickness longed for the city that has you in it. From far away a deadly vapor struck me, just such as the mouths of Hades breathe. For Empedocles, Strife produces neckless heads; but here the rule of non-rule has produced countless necks upon countless necks, all without heads. What Achridian is not a headless neck, knowing how to honor neither God nor man?

I have been condemned to live among such monstrosities. And the worst thing is that there is no hope that these necks will ever be furnished with heads by any stronger powers, as the friendship of the sage of Acragas brought incomplete forms toward completion. My hopes have wholly flowed away. An eagle goes on foot upon the scepter of Zeus, alas, and is caught in the mud, and endures the croaking of frogs.

They think it dreadful, and unworthy of the fortunes of their time, if they do not luxuriate in every way upon the bird of Zeus. They mount upon his back, full of filth and insolence; and once mounted, they cry out something ugly and out of tune. What else should be expected from a marshy soul? They seem to sing some sort of victory paean. But the eagle's beak has not yet been blunted, nor have the points of his talons. They can still tear and strip flesh. Yet he considers retaliation and vengeance a greater outrage to himself than the outrage they commit, and so he looks only toward the rays of the sun, more fixedly and clearly, as nature fitted his eyes to do. It seems that he makes the sun, whoever that sun may be, a witness of his suffering, and does the same thing: shaming it, and showing that such a creature's being mocked by such things is a matter for indignation.

Then, by the pure rays of that sun, with which you yourself are daily illumined and illumine those whom you beget as eagle-like, you most philosophical and most gracious of men: does this insulted eagle long in vain to live with you among the blessed, where the sky lies wide and clear, after being dragged out of mud and mire? I do not think you will say so. Rather, may you put to sleep the lake of evils here and the frogs that are its offspring. I have nothing worse to pray upon you than that you should feast on the familiar pain of gout.

You may think I am jesting in this way; yes, by the Muses of eloquence, because your graces are so many, and because even remembering them so loosens my grief that it leaves its mark on my letters to you. At such moments I am released from every sorrow and borne freely toward your graces by other graces. But may God, the true giver of grace, grant me one day either complete release from the evils here or at least consolation; and may he grant you complete release from the pitiless beast, or at least respite. For surely I have not alone missed the road that leads to him, nor has he given only to me an unmixed cup to drink.
