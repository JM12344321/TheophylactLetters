## Revised English Translation

My holy lord: if your height too has forgotten my lowliness, and does not even wish to learn how things stand with us, this is an addition to my misfortunes. Now truly those who are making war on my heaven have piled Ossa upon Pelion, or, if you like, the Giants have set a crown upon the tower of Chalanes. If, however, you do keep us in memory and ask about our affairs, lend me your ears for a little while: learn a few things, and infer from them what has been left unsaid.

The myths make Heracles, glorious by Zeus and by the Fates, the slave of Omphale. Omphale was queen of the Lydians, a woman soft, rich in gold, wishing to seem most beautiful, insulted if anyone compared her with a rose, disdaining even the arms of Hera, the hair of Leto, the ankles of Hebe, and the feet of Thetis, wishing in every respect to be one image of Aphrodite.

While the victorious hero served her, he reclined among the Lydian women and carded purple wool, and this work filled him with panic. Sometimes he prepared stews; at other times he handed around the cup, gripping it with great and noble fingers that trembled because they were so unpracticed in wine-serving. For this he was struck with a golden sandal, and, handed over to a Lydian eunuch slave as overseer, he had trouble learning the cupbearer's art. His fingers longed for the club. The eunuch even plucked the beard of his pupil, for, being a eunuch, he was at war with hair. There grief helped Heracles in accomplishing the task. It wasted away his fingers, and carrying the cup required thinner ones.

Nor was this all for the hero born after three nights. He also shared the work of the women grinding grain. He hummed along with the female millers; and when they tuned up something shrill and broken, he answered in a rough, straight tone. They burst out laughing, clapped their hands, left the work, and ordered him alone to turn the mill like an unrhythmic donkey. The son of Zeus suffered these things and endured that outrage until the appointed times released him from slavery. Then he labored again, but with deeds worthy of Zeus and of his journeying, for he purified the world of its disorders.

I have written you my own portrait in the condition where I now am. The difference is only this: I am not serving a rich, clean, beautiful queen, altogether a golden Aphrodite, but barbarian slaves, unclean men reeking of sheepskins, poorer in life than they are rich in malice, or rather kings at once in poverty of life and in malice altogether.

Release me from this shameful slavery, if you can; otherwise I shall go from you before God's hour because of my vexation, a son who has done nothing worthy of his father. That will be inglorious for me, and perhaps no less shameful for those who tolerate it.
