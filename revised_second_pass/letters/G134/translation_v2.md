## Revised English Translation

You are making a dolphin into a plough-ox by asking me now about an ecclesiastical question. If you do not know that the sea of Bulgaria has now refashioned me into such a creature, and that I am no longer that apostolic ox, the kind the Law ordered not to be muzzled while treading out the grain, then you will not know even yourself. Unless, of course, you do know this, and you also know that faith can do all things, and that it will not be powerless even for this: to restore the former ox from dolphin back into ox. Or else you would show yourself quite absurd if, seeing vice work monsters for the worse, you did not hope that virtue might work new things for the better, especially after hearing Truth say that the door will be opened to the one who knocks.

These are strong words, and now I shall answer you, because your shamelessness is worthy of respect.

We kiss the priest's shoulder during the services of the fast, when through him we partake of the mysteries. Perhaps we do so because of the greater reverence we display on such days, restraining and drawing ourselves in on every side and showing no bold familiarity or cheerfulness, things that naturally lead one to offer a kiss on the mouth. So too, when we wish reverently to kiss emperors or others who are otherwise eminent, we do not kiss them on the mouth, but on one or another part of the body, and we pay the debt of love with honor.

Since in the fasting days we humble ourselves, as the old saying has it, and afflict our souls because of him who humbled himself even to the most shameful death and gave his soul over to voluntary suffering, we preserve reserve and restraint toward all others, but especially toward priests. Avoiding the mouths, we bring our lips to the shoulders, both because they are, after the head, more honorable than the other parts, and because they are the beginnings of the hands that distribute to us the holy bread. We kiss those hands too; and on other days we do not necessarily confine the kiss to the shoulders, because those days are not days of affliction and restraint, but of openness and brightness, into which bold familiarity usually enters.

Perhaps, too, someone who loves to labor over the meaning, and who has learned to see, when Jesus is transfigured into greater brightness, not only his garment but also the outward covering of the Word made light-like, will be deemed worthy of the radiance of his face and will say that the shoulders are a symbol of practical action. Through that action we show that we bear the cross of the Lord and, as far as is possible, are crucified together with him.

Since, then, the fasts signify for us being crucified and dead together with Christ, we honor the shoulders that nobly bear the burden. We kiss those who show that the Lord's burden is light, and through those shoulders they often add themselves to him by going beyond the commandments: as those who spend themselves for the gospel from the work of their hands, though they have authority not to work; and as those who, though virginity was not imposed on them by law, nevertheless live as virgins.

So too the judges bind up the arms of wrestlers. They know that when one member rejoices, all the members rejoice with it, just as when one suffers, all suffer with it; yet they assign greater honor to the member that has labored more. Moreover, those who honor the priests' shoulders strengthen the priests and make them sturdier for bearing the burden. The kiss becomes for them something like the shout of encouragement for runners: the runners seem to grow wings by it, and priests receive the kiss as an exhortation to bear the burden steadfastly. Rather, those who kiss also urge themselves and others toward the same labors, since they see that labor according to God is honored.

In the fasting services the priest elevates the presanctified bread under the holy veil that covers it, the aer not being removed from between, but still held up by the hands of the deacons until the priest ceases elevating it. You asked us the reason for this too.

In my judgment, it is to indicate the strange newness of the divine economy, according to which what is glorified appears dishonored and what has been dishonored appears glorified. For the body of the Bread that came down from heaven was elevated: it was lifted on the cross, drawing all people upward, showing the way, triumphing over the principalities and powers and making an open example of them, and being glorified by the Father in the open air of creation. The miracles at the cross were no moderate glory. Yet it was covered over by the signs of dishonor: shown naked, nailed to the wood, given vinegar and gall to drink, blasphemed by those passing by and not walking the straight path, not even granted mercy by the thieves crucified with him, with whom he was crucified for still greater insult, and finally pierced with a lance, even if a greater glory came there through the blood and water that flowed together from the dead side. These were the coverings of glory so long as the elevation lasted.

After the cross, the marks of dishonor were removed and the glory of the resurrection was uncovered. The one who yesterday was naked, dishonored, and granted burial only by philanthropy rises; he shakes all things, is brightly proclaimed by bright angels, shows himself as having received authority in heaven and on earth, and is worshiped by those who had abandoned and denied him, even though they are witnessed not to have done this before, at least not in this way expressly and by the very word. And that very body that had been mocked, pierced through with nails, and stabbed with a lance is shown to be spiritual and incorruptible.

Even now, when it is elevated by the stewards of the mystery and shown to the initiated as holy and sanctifying, it nevertheless has a mysterious elevation and, to speak plainly, appears in a hidden way. For who can clearly understand how the flesh condemned to death, precisely in its being simply flesh, became life-giving through the Spirit, whether one means the divine nature to which it was hypostatically united, for the Word also knows how to call God spirit, or the Holy Spirit himself, whom we believe to have become the anointing of that flesh? And how does what is broken and distributed, eaten and spent, deify me? These things are hidden now, so long as the elevation and manifestation of the mystery are carried out by human beings, and in this way we have not yet wholly escaped symbols.

But when we no longer celebrate the divine rites through human beings, and the Word himself drinks with us the new cup of the kingdom and reveals himself to those worthy of seeing him directly and worthy of the kingdom, then every covering is removed. What is now darkness, having put a hidden veil over itself, will be seen face to face, in clear form and not through riddles. And just as we passed from shadow to image, so from image we shall pass to naked truth.

What then will you say? "This custom is not observed in the other seasons of the sacred rites, but only in the fasting services." Yes, good friend, because the manner of those rites is different. In those rites the bread must first be sanctified and then elevated, and sanctification finds it already uncovered beforehand, not still covered together with the veil. Why? Because it is sanctified through the visitation of the Spirit, and to him all things are naked, with neither curtain nor covering. In the services during the fasts, however, the bread is brought in and set forth already sanctified. It is therefore fitting that different rites have different things performed in them.

Perhaps what is done then is also an admonition to the workers of virtue during the fasts. When they are lifted up, setting their minds on the things above, where Christ is, and pressing on toward the commonwealth in heaven so that they may become fellow-citizens of the saints, friends of God, and holy, as he too is holy, they should be lifted up beneath the veil of obscurity and humility: not staging their fast by gloomy appearance, not publishing their prayer, not trumpeting their almsgiving, but crushing the spirit, and especially at that moment understanding that they are earth and ashes, when they are about to converse with divine and better things. They should bear fruit for the one who knows hidden things, with whom everything deposited is laid up safely, not lying exposed naked to those who look and those who plunder, nor released to the winds so as to be scattered by them.

These things come to you from us who once trod the grain. If you see wheat in them, know that the grace belongs to the one who has now refashioned me too into my ancient comeliness, as he once refashioned Adam. But if the whole thing is straw and chaff, such are the harvests of dolphins; curse the sea through which we lost the ox.

## Source And Revision Notes

- Gautier's title identifies the recipient as Theophylact's brother Demetrios.
- The letter answers two liturgical questions: the Lenten shoulder-kiss of the priest and the elevation of the presanctified bread while covered by the aer.
- Major allusions include Deuteronomy 25:4, Luke 11:10, Philippians 2:8, Matthew 17:2, Galatians 5:24, John 12:32, Colossians 2:15, John 19:34, Matthew 28:18, John 6:63, John 4:24, 1 Corinthians 13:12, Hebrews 10:1, Colossians 3:1-2, Philippians 3:20, Ephesians 2:19, Matthew 6:2/6:16, Genesis 18:27, and Daniel/Susanna 42.
