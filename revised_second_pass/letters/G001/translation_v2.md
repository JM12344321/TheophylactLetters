## Revised English Translation

Where, then, are those who reproach us with severity, those who, through irrationality, triumph over reason, those truly disorderly accusers of good order? Come here, shameless and contemptuous tongue, sharp sword against good things. Come, restrain your rush a little, if indeed you can, and give us too a season to speak. If ears have been given you by nature, listen. But if you have been mutilated by nature and habit so that you cannot hear the voice of an enchanter or be treated by a wise physician, then at least grant silence to those who do listen, so that we may uncover your shame.

As for me, my intelligent audience, I am a shoot from that blessed Greece of old, transplanted here under the pretext of education. I do not know what promise my parents saw in me, though they fed themselves on good hopes for me. When by divine grace I had received an introduction worthy of those hopes, I learned that this great house, filled with every ordered structure and composition, is conceived and called a kosmos, and that it does not depart from being what it is until it departs from being a kosmos; but if anyone were to practice rebellion in it, its constitution would immediately be dissolved.

These things persuaded me while I was still nourished on the milk of myth: that much-talked-of Phaethon, more famous for incompetence than for birth, and even more famous for the enormity of his disaster than for his incompetence, since he was no less foolish than a son of the Sun and more unfortunate in falling from the chariot than skillful upon it; and again a flood that destroyed the earth in another way and preserved Deucalion, remnant of the former world and seed of the second. But when I had passed beyond childhood and was coming already among men, I no longer had myth as teacher but the truth of things itself.

I found that the seasons, when they are well blended, grant us every grace and eagerly reproduce for us the life under Kronos, that painless, golden, blessed life. But if they shift even a little from their appointed order, alas for calamity upon calamity. One man laments his field, scowls over his labors, mourns his sweat, and at one moment curses drought, at another savage rain, now the burning heat, now hail. Another groans over his vines and harvests only this in abundance: even if he picks some cluster, it is that cluster of bitterness from Scripture that he picks; and if he steps into the winepress, it drips many tears. He presses, but it is his soul he presses; he is drunk, but from bile, and he does not have the consolation Solomon gives to those in mourning. I have seen a tree shedding its leaves; I pitied its condition and assigned the misfortune to the seasons.

What should I say of the body, that resident corpse, that tomb carrying itself, to say something a little novel? Is it not, so long as it has the good disposition and mixture of the elements, sound and at the same time exactly what it is called? But when one of the elements changes its order, health too is transposed; illnesses visit human beings, some by day and some by night, of their own accord; the doctor is at the door, and perhaps not only the doctor, but herbs in plenty, drugs, the belly softened like a skin-bag, fingers on the pulse, and Galen on the lips. What next? The undertaker succeeds the doctor, the grave succeeds the bed, and tears succeed the more cheerful promises.

I see houses too standing firm when the slave is a slave, bends the neck, does not rebel against the master, and knows where the scales of fortune tilt for him. Nor are the good laws of cities preserved otherwise than when the ruling element holds the place of mind and orders everything, while the ruled element is guided and takes its rank; one commands and the other acts, one goads and the other moves.

If one must also examine ancient histories, I consider the city of Athens prosperous when it was ordered more decorously. But once the Alcibiadeses and Calliases appeared, with banquets of insolence, dances out of mysteries, Aristophanes and the theater, the one introducing philosophy as a comic object and the other receiving it, Lysander prospered against the Athenians, the lock of Greece was shorn off, and thirty criminals trampled upon the fallen state. I consider the Spartans too: before Lycurgus set them in order they fared very badly, but after they were established by Lycurgus' laws and words, they heard the finest praises and shone in their affairs. Looking even to the cranes, I see that they were teachers of Palamedes and an archetype of formation, through which our armies too are preserved. These things, and more than these, I learned from good order.

So it seems I was ignorant of the barbarous and bizarre laws that you yourself bring against me after reading them from your own tablets. Consider the charges made against us: "The teacher," you say, "sent away from school students who, because of youth, had erred and done something unworthy of letters. We would never have dared such a thing." Of course you would not: you are not a teacher but a corrupter and criminal of youth. I, however, do not repent when I cut out wickedness. You might even pay a fee to get people to hear you; I stand above those who pay fees. You will flatter; I might even strike. I shall not be found using words of flattery, so that I too may boast with Paul. You will show humanity beyond measure; I shall show hatred of evil, without rejecting love for human beings.

And you seem distressed that we do not put up with people striking us on the head, but show them something Hellenic, masculine, and bearing the imprint of ancient nobility. Either you yourself were suited to our badly behaved students, or else your utter wickedness hides itself behind my moderate faults. Perhaps you will accuse fathers too of often forgetting even nature for the sake of nature, and showing sharp anger from the nose, now striking, now barring the doors in their children's faces and excluding them from the table. Yet they are fathers, and I am a teacher, even if you burst ten thousand times saying the opposite.

Then again, if my rejection had been irreconcilable, my threads impossible to turn back, the beam of my balance tilted only to the left, and my school had only night, like some land told in myth, you still would have had nothing to say, though you would have seemed to. But now, though I know I am speaking to a blind man, lift your eyes round about and see my children gathered. See: I heal the broken and receive the rejected. I turned back the wandering and sought the lost. The one who for a time was not my people, I call my people. By joining to my sons those who had limped a little from the paths and become strangers, I plant groans in you. I struck, and again I shall heal. I chastened, but did not hand them over to death. With the left hand I pushed them away, but with the right I drew them to myself. My tongue renounced them, but my mind enrolled them as heirs.

But you kept saying to me, "Well done, well done," indulging the desire of your soul and feasting on hopes, nourished by what seemed to you decay. You will never taste anything good; nothing pleasant and delightful will draw your appetite. You lie in wait for rotten things, stand over what is stale, and belch out words worthy of your food, more polluted than vultures and more disgusting than mice. Yet I am left wondering at your absurdity, because it is truly worth wonder. If we are acting well, why accuse what is being done? If it does not seem good to you, why rejoice over what is not good? You must choose one of two things: either to be a wicked judge or an envious watcher. Toward those whose best actions you blame, you have proved no fair judge; by the worst things on which you feed, you have shown yourself wicked.

Come then, wrap yourself in shame as in a cloak, and put on disgrace as a tunic, so that we may clothe you in garments from Scripture, woven from above. Receive the leaden stone in your mouth, like Iniquity in Zechariah, and let your deceitful lips become speechless.

We were weak for a little while, but we have been strengthened. We dozed, but we rose up like strong men. Hezekiah fell sick, but received an addition of years. The servant of the synagogue ruler was ill, but was healed by a word alone and from afar; so too the royal official's servant was made better by the Life of all. This is how I understand God's care for us. I am not yet speaking of men raised from tombs on the fourth day, of others being carried out and leaping up from the bier, of a dead little girl awakened as if from sleep, and before these, of women receiving their dead by resurrection, or of a prophet seeing bare bones, flesh growing again, skin coming over them, and hair springing up.

Such are my misfortunes: a father striking children and then kissing them again, shutting them out of the house and then receiving them again. But you, list your own affairs for me. No, you are ashamed of them and say to the hills, "Cover me," and to the mountains, "Fall on me." Why then do you pick at scraps like those starving at the very edge of famine? How charming you are, and noble in stupidity, if you accuse others of distortion when you yourself are blind, of lacking a thumb when you yourself are handless, and if you reproach Menelaus for his wandering to Egypt when you yourself have often measured out Charybdis, encountered beast-like men, armed yourself against man-like beasts, Laestrygonians and Cyclopes, and Scylla, unlike in nature but like herself in wickedness.

"What shall I do with you, Ephraim?" God somewhere cries out in distress through Hosea. What medicine shall I apply to your envy? "There is no applying bandages, nor oil, nor dressing," Isaiah answers; and "your wound is painful," Jeremiah says again. You will therefore be left untreated, like the daughter of Zion, or rather like Babylon, which after being treated was found incurable. Perhaps we shall even follow the medical laws that tell us not to lay hands on those mastered by disease.

But for you, children, what else should I now advise except not to gladden our enemies by what we do, not to set choice foods before their wickedness? Why do you forge weapons for your enemies? Why sharpen their sword? Why train that criminal band against yourselves? Give them no occasion, and it is dead. Not even Balaam could curse the one whom God did not curse. The Jews thirsted to put Christ to death, but he gave them no cause; he was not convicted concerning sin, and Pilate acquitted him. Make yourself orderly and you will not be put to shame before those outside. Grieve the enemy by becoming noble and good. Nothing bites the envious tribe so much as an enemy held in good repute and lodged in praising mouths. If something hard befalls you, he applauds; if fortune blows fair and the voyage is auspicious, he sinks into dejection.

Present your life to your enemies in this way: arrange it so that your way of living is admired more than other people's speech, or rather your speech more than your life, and by the one you defeat others, by the other even yourselves, or rather by both you defeat everyone. Thus your way of life will stand before those who wish to look as a pure virgin, without spot or wrinkle. Thus the adversary will be put to shame, finding nothing in you, as the divine apostle says. And may the Word, who made all things in wisdom, as we have heard and believe, the God of order and not of disorder or confusion, equip us in every good work, so that, living in peace with ourselves and with all, we may be one, bound together by the bond of love, and may be trusted as disciples and ambassadors of the Word.
