## Revised English Translation

I ought, children, to speak in a more cheerful and auspicious way, and always to display some graceful face of discourse, so that I may not seem gloomy, ill-omened, and harsher than Timon. But speech seems to be the shadow of things: it receives its particular shape from them, rises upright when they rise, bends down with what bends down, sits together with what reclines, and in short takes its form from them. Since bitter things are always falling upon me, my speech too must be reported in the more bitter direction. We shall not play comedy on tragic themes, nor dance over a funeral song; the Trojan women did not sing a wedding song over dead Hector. Give me a springtime of subjects, and I shall display in return the flowers of speech. But so long as you bring on a winter of affairs, do not be vexed by the harshness of my words. Since you dare to do what is not good, endure now what is not friendly.

For me, children, whether someone calls this madness, since perhaps it is madness to undertake the cure of the incurable, or calls it zeal and forethought for what is noble, from the day I received this much-envied chair, which many labors built together for me under God as architect, a sharp and unquenchable passion fell upon me: to bring rhetoric back to its ancestral condition. Its truth had been driven into exile, and only a certain phantom was living as citizen, like the phantom of Herakles that the poets introduce in Hades. Thus Plato's saying has now become clear, when he called a certain kind of rhetoric a phantom of a part of politics.

When simple, noble language, language most significant in relation to its subject, was thought to be rusticity, the offspring and mark of a dry and hard character; when refinement, glitter, and swollen polish of words, and in general what is unhealthy and hollow, seemed to be urbanity and education; when thinking something manly, analyzing a political question, combing through books of the ancients, making some needed inquiry, peering from the apparent to what is hidden, borrowing some things and discovering others besides, when all this seemed to be empty craft and the man occupied with it truly idle; but adorning some shallow little thought with many-colored coverings, composing from marketplace and trampled-over ideas a luxury that seduces adolescents, and preening oneself on rare words was judged the most approved end of the art: hearing these things, I was disturbed, and seeing them, I was shaken. Both what was said and what was done seemed to me worthy of indignation. I often addressed speech itself on behalf of speech, crying, "Rescue the sons of the Achaeans from the mist, and give them clear sky to see." Whether it will ever give that, I do not know; at least it has given this present occasion.

If some of us do not use well what has been given, let the blind man not blame the sun, nor the eunuch accuse the woman of barrenness, nor the man without melody or rhythm charge Pindar's lyre with discord. Many, many indeed, both perceived and enjoyed the benefit: some were grafted from wild olive into cultivated olive; others were planted as cultivated olives from the start and received no bitter root at all. Their fruit is noble and able to fill the mouth of the eater, not slack, not merely sweet all through, no, by the treatises of the ancients and the characters they handed down to me, but well blended and showing great health.

Others remain as they were at the beginning. They have altered neither their speech nor their character, but make the land useless; though cultivated, they are untilled. Worse, they corrupt as many of the others as they can. Terrible, terrible is malice, especially when it is established in children, a tender thing, and becomes not a passing disposition but a settled habit, and is transferred into nature, the more violent thing. The child can no longer rise up out of evil and choose the better; he is soaked into what he was brought up with, captive to habit, remaining enslaved to what yoked him from the start and clinging to those doctrines as if they were ancestral. To speak more plainly, he holds fast to lessons learned from infancy more tightly than little babies hold to the breast, and therefore, always rubbing shoulders with those of his own age, he passes on the evil more easily.

Since here below nature is to conquer worse things, for the things above are divine, unshaken, and wholly blessed around God, while the things below and around us have been allotted disturbance, tossing, and storms, and so every form of wickedness too, as if it were not lawful for evils to dwell or be nourished anywhere except in the regions of matter, in my judgment this is so that we may hate matter fervently, being struck and pressed by it on every side and tasting its wickedness, and, looking to the homeland above, may strive to raise it as far as possible. By remolding and reshaping the body of matter, as craftsmen work what is still ugly and formless, and by becoming gods within the world, creating a new creation in ourselves and beautifying the unseen and unformed earth into form and order, we shall thus at last become also above the world.

The Word, the Law, and the great mystery of Christ are capable of these things. Yet each thing is powerful in its own sphere. For that reason the affairs of my school share in the worldly nature too: it is not yet calm, nor has it become one through the victory of the better. For these reasons I curse the day on which I was born, my birth from sin, for sin, and in sins, and I lament that my mother bore me. Why did she show me labors and pains? Why did she keep me until this time? Was it so that I might look upon rich wickedness in poor vessels, and the tower of Chalanne being built no longer by those giants, whose bodily size and arrogance towered above the clouds, but by half-grown youths, incomplete in age but complete in malice, pygmies in body but colossal in wickedness, young in appearance but old in villainy, students of the art but teachers of evil craft?

If you give them milk to drink, you have given them asp poison; if you feed them honey, what is eaten becomes bitterness. They will not be assimilated to what you give; rather, they will assimilate what they receive, or else revile those who offer it. They eat my bread but magnify a stumbling-block against me. They seem to be men of peace, but they are sons of Ephraim, stringing the bow and shooting. They cry, "Hail, Rabbi," and run up to kiss, but they betray by night. They put a cloak on you, but mock you too. They bend the knee, but strike too. They give a scepter, but spit too. They proclaim you king, but number you with the lawless. They honor with the lips, but their heart stands far from ours.

Do we say something playful to newcomers? "The rhetor has gone soft." Do we treat some a little more sharply? "The rhetor is graceless. He seats so-and-so beside him; he converses more with this one. Did you not see that he looked gently toward that one? We are rhetorical Cimmerians, sunless in that we are not granted even a small glance. To so-and-so when he asked a question he lent his ear and gave his tongue, but to us not even his eyes." But you do not ask, my fine fellow. You are thinking what you will report about us to the enemies when they ask you. You do not trade in useful things; you go after the more rotten ones. You do not gather the pearl; you boast of trampling it with your drinking companions. We do not class you as one wishing to be a student, but as a plotter against those who do. Your mouth is always overflowing with malice, your tongue entangles deceit, your crooked heart devises evils, and at every moment you stir up trouble. David and Solomon painted you beforehand.

Ah, what sort of people I was benefiting without knowing it; what vipers I was warming without realizing it; what creatures I was raising on milk and cakes. Foolish creature that I am, exposed to deceivers: I did not learn from one, did not learn from two, was not made wiser by three or four, but still fall upon wickedness unguarded and unfortified. I did not know that we are a race harder than Hesiod's iron race, nor that children now can mock aged fathers more shamelessly. Otherwise I too would gladly have uttered that prayer: "Would that I no longer had any part among these, but had either died before or been born later."

Yet even these things are light and bearable. It is not surprising that those nursed on wickedness from youth love their nurse. But now those whom I begot, whom I raised, to whom I gave even my own soul to possess, I have lately found baring their malice, for they bare it even if they spread many coverings over it. Hear, heaven, and give ear, earth: I begot and raised sons, but they rejected me and go about to strangers, to those not strangers, to friends, to those not friends, dragging my affairs through the mud. As for me, that is nothing. Let them strike, pierce, and spare me not; I receive gladly the blows of my children. They do not wound, for they are children. They do not grieve me, for I am a father. At the same time I provide them an example of how much patience one must show toward the dearest people when they insult, so that if someday they are struck either by us or by others, they may not be vexed as though struck by friends. Besides, I think I shall shame them when they are overcome by our kindness, they by whom we are overcome. For when they see that the man whom they sin against if they do not honor him in every way bears their insults, will they not feel shame?

Another man might perhaps have answered the insults and gone further. He would have punished some, prevented others from attending, defended himself openly before all, and wiped away the grounds on which, with what ingratitude, he is slandered as partial. The defense would certainly be easy, since it rests on the accusation itself. Because we cast our vote with the greater part, perhaps also the more intelligent, perhaps even the more approved in speaking, and agreed to explain the book On Forms, for this we are partial and loudly denounced in the marketplaces. These are things for another man to suffer and do, perhaps a small-souled, small-minded man who lives by the opinions of donkeys.

May education never have such a grip on me that it depends on little boys whether I am grieved or not grieved, whenever they wish. I stand unbending toward such things and despise the opinion of the many. If some laugh at me, I am not thereby laughed down; that is the noble saying of Diogenes. Let them be disposed toward us, then, however they wish. But to behave toward these companions as toward enemies, to undertake an undeclared war so irrationally and over empty things, to bite constantly with hidden bites and shoot constantly with arrows from the dark: this, in my judgment, is the last insolence. This discredits me before everyone. I seem to be a teacher of irrational creatures, a doctor who does not remove diseases but brings them in, preparing antidotes that are not releases from sufferings but poisons, and a vine-dresser of thorns, like the one lamenting in Isaiah.

Why, foolish ones, do you slaughter one another inhumanly and most pitiably, when you ought to sharpen the sword of reason according to Hesiod's good strife? You do not sharpen reason, far from it; you sharpen unreason. If you defended yourselves rhetorically against one another, becoming craftsmen of words nobly and well, even that would be terrible, for how is it not terrible to use against one's own people the things meant for one's own people? Still, the exercise might have seemed a display of youthful ambition, since Plato permits the young men of the same republic to tear at one another with dialectical mouths. But now it is utterly foolish insults that one composes against another.

And yet I am ashamed to share in the disgrace. It is not because they babble such things that we too must publish them; rather, because even they should not say them, we too should keep silent. Nevertheless, we shall set out in public the shamefulness of the words, so that they may know what sort of profit they get from their age and how they wither it by the things through which they strive to flourish in evil. Look how much longer that one's stride is than Ajax's; that other's is more sluggish than a tortoise. Do you see how straight and taut this fellow walks? His thoughts are loftier than a Persian tiara, and he considers everyone ants or gnats. When he has to ask a question, he sets his eyes like horn or iron; his nostril breathes insolence and contempt. But did you hear what sort of question he asked? He asked the teacher about a word. Will you not take yourself off to the workshops of elementary education, so that the grammarians may advance you in the meanings of words and at the same time laugh together as they sit there, not even separated from each other in their chairs, that strange company, those who bear shields together under Hermes?

Someone writes, practicing often, and sharpens his tongue with the tools forged by the art. But where is this boundless talker, this Dodonaean oracle? Let us at once nod assent and grant that the works are yours: may you enjoy them so. For it is not we, but you, fools, who will give birth to such a thing. Therefore what you cannot do, you do not wish for others either; what you do not wish, you do not even suppose; and what you do not suppose, you are not persuaded of. For each man supposes what he wishes, and one's own passion counts greatly toward assent. So that I may not stir the mud, be disgusted myself, and disgust you, such things, and worse than these, are your fine studies and exercises.

Such are the pupils whom fairest rhetoric receives back from us, turned in the opposite direction from my intention and choice. As I said at the beginning, I was eager to improve the young in character and in teaching, erasing whatever bad writings and hearings they had from hand and tongue, and writing in their place whatever seemed to me better and preserving nobility. But the impressions were ineradicable and immovable, so that they became for many of the others patterns of wickedness. They made their ears heavy so as not to hear, set their heart in disobedience, and hardened their faces beyond stone. Zechariah and Isaiah justly avenge you. As it seems, there is in the kosmos some allotment of an envious demon, who always fastens on good things and eats away the roots, either not allowing the shoot to emerge or hastening to wither it after it has emerged. It is not lawful for a foreign plant to sprout in his gardens. This one makes my sweats fruitless. I vote to restore rhetoric; he votes against me the rhetor, prevails with judges who lack judgment, drowns out my voice without even speaking, and is a silent chatterer.

What then shall we do? Shall we become soft when we have such a rival in the craft? Shall we lay down our weapons and serve a slavery pitiful and painful? That would grant him the greatest favor. We shall persist, then, in our original principles. We shall fight as men about to die, and we shall not die. Why should I not persuade this or that man, as Leonidas persuaded the trembler? Come, lift your eyes round about, unless you have altered them by envy, and see my young olive plants, planted around my table, rooted by the outlets of the waters, which will also give their fruit in season. I shall not cease digging around them, watering and cleansing them as far as I can, and if ever necessary, adding also the manure of dishonor and rebuke, and above all setting my own manner before them, a thing more forceful for persuasion.

Unless someone indicts me as over-proud, and for that reason foolish, as wise in my own eyes and knowledgeable before myself and puffed up beyond human measure, I do not praise the meddlesomeness and disorder of the many, nor do I feast myself on other people's lives. Rather, I have diverted the investigative and meddlesome part of my nature toward the ancient wise men. Though it is somewhat burdensome to say, I shall say it because I am compelled: few of the ancient works have escaped me. Whether you name something technical, scientific, or historical, I know the aim of each. If I have not devoted myself to all of them together, I nevertheless preside over one work. What is that? Say it, you envious ones. I received Sparta as my lot. With God I adorn her, and I shall adorn her with glory hereafter. If I have any power of conjecture, sharpness, and foresight of what is ahead, I know clearly that the future time will make even me blessed and desired.

In short, I practice Plato's justice by doing my own business, and according to Herodotus' Muse I look to my own affairs; or, if you prefer, according to Socrates' judgment I inquire what evil and good has happened in my halls, and I preserve Cleanthes' cowardice as mother of erring less. Whence I have this opinion, I do not know: perhaps from prudence, perhaps from simplicity. Listen: I think that those who examine and meddle in others' affairs have nothing of their own in which they could delight, since otherwise they would not turn toward the evils of others; and those who seek what slander is said about this or that man will have nothing praiseworthy said about themselves. For the man who attends to others' affairs must necessarily neglect his own, since not even the eye stands watch over its own and over foreign things at the same time.

Be disposed like this yourselves, and perhaps you will no longer blame the methods of my teaching. Instead, each of you will set me up as a benefactor on monuments that do not stand still, monuments that do not decay, but travel everywhere and are made immortal together with the immortal thing of souls. Let these few perish, one or two, whoever of the Achaeans counsel apart; there will be no accomplishment for them. And if some Thersites should make rebellion, hated by the kings, his back will be beaten with the scepter of Odysseus, and he will be driven in this worthless state from the sober assembly.
