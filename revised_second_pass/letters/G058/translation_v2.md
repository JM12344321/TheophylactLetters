## Revised English Translation

The present old man came before us, most holy brother, as we were entering the church, while both ecclesiastical and lay officials were present. Breaking into lamentation, full of groaning and pity, he related his whole story from beginning to end: how you became angry with him around the month of July; how, when he came to us, at the time when you yourself were also staying here, he explained what he had suffered; how we, showing nothing judicial or more authoritative toward your love, asked you in a loving and brotherly way simply to have humane regard for the old man and to correct whatever had happened against him that caused distress; how you yourself promised henceforth to show him every kindness in both deed and word; and how far the fulfillment of those promises fell short, so that, unable to bear the weight of the great kindness supposedly being shown to him, he ran to our mighty and holy emperor, despairing of the archiepiscopal cure - or rather condemning it as not only useless for healing but even as scraping open the wounds. He did indeed gain access to the height of that power and receive remedies effective so far as the giver was concerned.

Then what? He said that your hand did not allow the imperial medicines to work, but drove him from every border of Triaditza and put him under the bonds of suspension from liturgy. After all this he begged us again to make one more trial of your love toward us. He said he knew that no good would come from this second approach to us, only greater evils; nevertheless he was blocking every road of accusation and condemnation.

Those present, as I said, were deeply distressed and found it grievous if our request on behalf of an old man suffering injustice had been so insulted - a request in which goodwill mediated authority. They said: whose soul, even a Pharaoh's, would not have been touched by an authority that restrained itself, put love forward, and did not command as from condemnation but gently entreated as from request? They added that one must now proceed more strictly against this insult, so that the one who insulted might learn not to give every rein to the self-will of error, nor to think beyond what one ought to think, but to know that God is a God of order, not of disorder and confusion.

They spoke of the divine powers arranged one under another and keeping the measure allotted by grace, whether grace stationed them thus or illuminated them thus; and even of the apostate powers fitted together in a kind of order with one another, from which their coherence arises, although in other respects they are disorderly and disharmonious. This is clear, they said, from Paul's placing rulers of the air and naming principalities and powers. They said that action should be taken against the insult not only for the sake of this old man, but simply for the sake of many others, because, they said, the bishop seems to display such arrogant and violent conduct toward not a few others as well. All these should be restrained by the example of the old man's case. For hardly anyone less reputable, and not easily worthy of such trust, but suspected perhaps of wrongdoing, would approach an archbishop when oppressed by a bishop, if he saw that one so devout and worthy of pity had reaped such treatment from coming to the archbishop.

They asked me also: "What would your beloved bishop of Triaditza have done if you had handed someone over to him for punishment? Clearly he would have honored him with the highest honors at his disposal. For the man who treated the one mediated for him - the one whose former sufferings were to be cured - with still greater and heavier harm, would certainly have benefited the man condemned by you. Yet how did he himself act toward the enemies of truth, we mean his own accusers, who deserved the serpents that destroyed the murmurers in the wilderness? Did he not act cheerfully, paternally, in short evangelically, as he learned from the Teacher who prayed to the Father even for those who crucified him?"

When I smiled gently at this, they said, "We understand why you bear the insult so patiently and gracefully. You love this noble man like a king, since you are insulted by him as by a brother." But I quelled their agitation with the words I had to use, speaking the Gospel itself: "Does our law judge a man unless it first hears him and knows what he is doing?" There, then, I did not believe that you had treated the old man contrary to what you had promised us. For that would plainly be an insult, and we would not readily be persuaded that you insulted us unless we thought you mad.

Yet we judged that we must write again about this to your holiness, so that, taking pity on the old age of the monk, sparing his condition, and looking to these times, which magnify gnats into camels, you may allow the old man to breathe out his soul in the monastery, since it hangs only at the edge of his lips. If there are any revenues, let him enjoy and administer them, since he is going to render account for them to the common Judge of all. So too, when we entrusted him with handling ecclesiastical revenues, we did not think that his accounts should be subjected to human days, except perhaps in matters that carry obvious condemnation and scandalize the many; we thought and said, believed and persuaded, that God alone is the auditor.

But if, flattering ourselves, we sit as bitter judges of others, there is danger that we shall be ranked with those who bind heavy and unbearable burdens but are unwilling to move them with their finger; or that, aged in evil days, we shall be condemned by our own lips; or that, as evil servants, we shall be judged by our own mouth; and again, that because we have not forgiven a little, though forgiven much, we shall be condemned.

These are the seeds of the Word from our husbandmen to you, most honored brother. May you prove, as we hope and pray, to be good soil, not rocky and thorny, and bear for us the fruit of obedience, so that you may be known as a perfect disciple of Christ, who came down from heaven not to do his own will but the will of the Father who sent him. But if even now you set us aside, you know whom you set aside through us, and to him you will have a most fearful account.

You will set us aside if, first, you do not restore the monastery to the monk, as the emperor also decreed, and loose him from the bond of suspension from liturgy. Then you must present openly to the synod the charges against him. This is necessarily owed by you both because of the suspended judgment concerning Lipenion, which must be completed by those who suspended it, of whom you yourself are one, and because of the votes that very rightly await us, as I wrote before. Then you will accept what appears to the brotherhood after examination.

You will anger, then, the one whom, as he himself said, you set aside through us. But besides this, you will find the canons themselves looking on you not cheerfully and not as if they could be disregarded, but gathering the cloud of their brows and condemning something violent, whether a rainstorm such as Moses and Aaron once innovated against a hardened king, or simply hail. For the one who despises kindness stores up wrath for himself, as we have learned. Since you bound this man with suspension from liturgy and made him without communion to you, the Spirit who spoke through those assembled at Carthage will lay on you yourself the bond of non-communion, unless just repentance looses the unjust bond.
