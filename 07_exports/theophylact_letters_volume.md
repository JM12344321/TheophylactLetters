# G004 Translation

Status: translated_from_pg_ocr_needs_gautier_collation

## Metadata

- Gautier ID: G004
- Recipient: the Lady Maria
- Old numbering: Lami-Finetti I
- Source: PG 126, public OCR packet PG093; aligned by GIBI index, p. 81

## English Translation

My holy lady and mistress: ever since I came from Achrida to the queen of cities, my many sins have fastened many griefs upon me. Yet, wretched as I am, I had not suffered anything like what has happened now: I was unable to render to your majesty my final and farewell act of reverence.

The earlier griefs your serenity lightened for me, or rather wiped away completely, so that not even a trace of them remained visible. Wonderful to say, they were changed into cheerfulness, transformed by your most divine right hand. Thus the words David spoke to God could also be spoken by me to your majesty: "According to the multitude of my pains in my heart, your consolations gladdened my soul."

But this present suffering: who will lighten it for my lowliness? Who will make it more bearable for my weakness? Only if I reckon upon your goodness shall I have consolation even in this. For I believe, as is reasonable, that you will receive this present letter as though it were that bodily audience which, against our will, we missed.

Let your majesty, who has mercy on me, know this. Returning from Nicomedia, for thither the waves of my troubles had carried me, I pressed the sailors many times, trying to make them turn the rudders and shift the sail toward your fortunate little island of Prinkipos. But they did not hear even the command in my forceful words, I suppose because a still more forceful cry, the cry of the north wind, was thundering in their ears. Coming down from the district of Panteichion, that bright wind made the crossing impassable unless we were to put in at Apollonias and then, after that, make our way safely to the great city. We did come through safely, though almost touching the land with our oars, so far were we kept from the open sea.

Often I wished to complete my desire and run to my only helper after God. But the murderous south wind struck against me in turn, as though it had made a compact with the north wind that each, by turns, should distress me with immeasurable blasts.

And then what happened? When permission for our departure was granted by the man entrusted with watching and correcting our affairs, the sebastos and praetor of Dyrrachion, my desire was once more scattered. This, I suppose, was to be expected; for "the desire of the sinner will perish." The enemy, ever envious of good things, accomplished everything he wished. Yet perhaps even his plans have been made useless, since he is the leader, indeed the maker and contriver, of sinners. For your majesty, as I hope, will receive this letter of mine in place of a farewell salutation and act of reverence, and will pardon the unworthiness of your servant, since the omission of my personal farewell was something I suffered rather than something I chose.

As for me, as though I had received what I had been eager to obtain and my deficiency had been supplied, I shall be disposed more cheerfully. Since here too they have unraveled for me the thread of teaching, may your holiness listen gently and kindly to this speechless speech, which uses the hand as its tongue; and may it itself plead for me with your goodness and protection.

Ask from God, before whom you stand each day through actions that lead toward him, a moderate experience for me in what remains to come. To ask for an end to afflictions would be too much. And show also what is properly your own: do not scatter your mercy away from me, but display, among your other gifts, your inborn and accustomed kindness toward me. Fulfill the promise which your truthful ministering mouth made some time ago to my fellow-servant and brother Demetrios.

I also have something playful to say to you, who are truly full of graces. While I have been in this city, our whole situation has grown stale and rotten, even the things that otherwise gave pleasure and fragrance. I am going down among the Bulgarians as a Constantinopolitan indeed, but in this strange condition, a Bulgarian reeking of decay, as they reek of the grease of their sheepskins. And the worst thing is that those people will think I have been found loathsome even by you. Otherwise my staleness would have been made fragrant, richly and graciously.

You know, then, what you must do, so that I may not be caught by those who have put me to shame. You will fumigate away the stench and condemn it to perpetual exile from me, striking its back with the very woods that the earth brings forth after receiving the first and pure rays of the sun. Then I shall be able to sing of you, in truth and not in flattery, those words which Solomon fitted to the bride in the Song, even if they are borne toward something greater and more mystical: "Your cheeks are like bowls of spices, yielding perfumes." For the command on my behalf will surely be brought forth through your cheeks.

These things, then, I have asked of your holy majesty somewhat in an old man's playful style. May the God to whom you belong, and from whom you are, himself rescue you from every evil and every injury, deem you worthy of his perfect gifts, make you the fragrance of Christ's good odor, lift you up to the altar above, and make you wholly a censer: so that your hand and action may offer whatever sacrifice they can provide, and you may send up the pleasant and acceptable incense compounded from the virtues.

## Translator's Notes

- The translation follows the PG/OCR text where readable; several phrases in the nautical section and the closing blessing are damaged and require Gautier collation.
- The "Bulgarians" passage is intentionally sharp and comic. I have preserved its unpleasant force rather than smoothing it into neutral complaint.

# G005 Translation

Status: translated_from_pg_ocr_needs_gautier_collation

## Metadata

- Gautier ID: G005
- Recipient: the Grand Domestic
- Old numbering: Lami-Finetti II
- Source: PG 126, public OCR packet PG094; aligned by GIBI index, p. 84

## English Translation

My holy lord: [the opening is damaged in the PG/OCR], and you seem to have forgotten my lowliness, or at least not even to have learned how things stand with me. Truly, those who once made war on all heaven piled Ossa upon Pelion, or, if you like, the giants set a crown upon the tower of Babel. If, however, you do keep us in memory and look about for news of our condition, lend me your ears for a little while: learn a few things, and infer the rest from what is left unsaid.

The myths make Heracles, glorious by Zeus and by the Fates, the slave of Omphale. Omphale was queen of the Lydians, a woman soft, rich in gold, wishing to seem most beautiful, insulted if anyone compared her with a rose, disdaining even the arms of Hera, the hair of Leto, the ankles of Hebe, and the feet of Thetis, wishing in every respect to be one image of Aphrodite.

While the victorious hero served her, he sat among the Lydian women and carded purple wool. Sometimes he stood near the storeroom and scattered the wool-baskets. At other times he handed around the cup, gripping it with great and noble fingers that trembled because they were so unpracticed in wine-serving. For this he was struck with a golden ring, and, handed over to a Lydian eunuch slave as tutor, he had trouble learning the cupbearer's art. His fingers longed for the club. The eunuch even plucked the beard of his pupil; being a eunuch, he was drawn toward hair. There grief helped Heracles in accomplishing the task. It wasted away his fingers, and carrying the cup became possible for thinner ones.

Nor was this all for the hero born after three nights. He also shared the work of the women grinding grain. He hummed along with the female millers; and when they tuned up something shrill and broken, he answered in a rough, straight tone. They burst out laughing, clapped their hands, left the work, and ordered him alone to turn the mill like an unrhythmic donkey. The son of Zeus suffered these things and endured that outrage until the appointed times released him from slavery. Then he labored again, but with deeds worthy of Zeus and of his journeying, for he purified the world of its disorders.

I have written you my own portrait in the condition where I now am. The difference is only this: I am not serving a rich, clean, beautiful queen, altogether a golden Aphrodite, but barbarian slaves, unclean men reeking of sheepskins, poorer in life than they are rich in malice, or rather kings at once in poverty of life and in malice altogether.

Release me from this shameful slavery, if you can; otherwise I shall die early, for Christ's sake, a son who has done nothing worthy of his father. That will be inglorious for me, and perhaps no less shameful for those who tolerate it.

So I set foot again in Achrida, and in my lovesickness longed for the city that has you in it. From far away a deadly vapor struck me, just such as the mouths of Hades breathe. For Empedocles, Strife produces neckless heads; but here the rule of non-rule has produced countless necks upon countless necks, all without heads. What Achridian is not a headless neck, knowing how to honor neither God nor man?

I have been condemned to live among such monstrosities. And the worst thing is that there is no hope that these necks will ever be furnished with heads by any stronger powers, as the friendship of the sage of Acragas brought incomplete forms toward completion. My hopes have wholly flowed away. An eagle goes on foot upon the scepter of Zeus, alas, and is caught in the mud, and endures the croaking of frogs.

They think it dreadful, and unworthy of the fortunes of their time, if they do not luxuriate in every way upon the bird of Zeus. They mount upon his back, full of filth and insolence; and once mounted, they cry out something ugly and out of tune. What else should be expected from a marshy soul? They seem to sing some sort of victory paean. But the eagle's beak has not yet been blunted, nor have the points of his talons. They can still tear and strip flesh. Yet he considers retaliation and vengeance a greater outrage to himself than the outrage they commit, and so he looks only toward the rays of the sun, more fixedly and clearly, as nature fitted his eyes to do. It seems that he makes the sun, whoever that sun may be, a witness of his suffering, and does the same thing: shaming it, and showing that such a creature's being mocked by such things is a matter for indignation.

Then, by the pure rays of that sun, with which you yourself are daily illumined and illumine those whom you beget as eagle-like, you most philosophical and most gracious of men: does this insulted eagle long in vain to live with you among the blessed, where the sky lies wide and clear, after being dragged out of mud and mire? I do not think you will say so. Rather, may you put to sleep the lake of evils here and the frogs that are its offspring. I have nothing worse to pray upon you than that you should feast on the familiar pain of gout.

You may think I am jesting in this way; yes, by the Muses of eloquence, because your graces are so many, and because even remembering them so loosens my grief that it leaves its mark on my letters to you. At such moments I am released from every sorrow and borne freely toward your graces by other graces. But may God, the true giver of grace, grant me one day either complete release from the evils here or at least consolation; and may he grant you complete release from the pitiless beast, or at least respite. For surely I have not alone missed the road that leads to him, nor has he given only to me an unmixed cup to drink.

## Translator's Notes

- This is a rhetorically dense complaint-letter, not a neutral report. The Heracles/Omphale exemplum and the eagle/frogs image are the argumentative core.
- The PG/OCR opening and a few mythological details are visibly damaged; the English preserves the secure movement of the passage and flags the damaged start.

## English Translation

But no word about me? You know well the little comic scrap.

Yet this office of yours, which has been allotted the task of doing good to many, forgets its own purpose in my case and deprives me alone of your benefits. For through your tongue it pours nectar in streams for everyone; but noble as it is, it leaves me dry, and, as the poets say, unwatered.

What advocate, then, will it appoint for itself? How will it not be convicted on every count when I summon it for wronging me? Does it take courage from my being made rustic among the barbarians? But, admirable mistress of the courts, since we are marshaled under your laws, we put our trust not in words but in facts; and the facts, surely, have not become rustic.

So steal a little of your tongue for me as well, and drip some of the nectar; or else sharpen that tongue still further in your own defense. For I, at least, will not endure failing to bring a charge against negligence in friendship. I am furnished with a sharp advocate: the facts. Even if my servant is weak and my horses are slow, Diomedes knows how to save Nestor.

- The closing allusion is Homeric: Diomedes rescues Nestor despite Nestor's slow horses and weakness.

- Old numbering: Lami-Finetti III

## English Translation

I did wish, in writing also to your holiness, to find leisure for long words and to set before your sympathetic eyes a broader stage for my tragedies. But since we are poor even in this, learn from the short letter addressed to our common friend, the Grand Domestic, how great and what sort of things we are suffering.

If the instruction you receive from him is sufficient for your understanding, become a teacher of our affairs to others as well. Nor will you need a fuller account from me. However you come to know the circumstances in which I am placed, make every effort to transform them by your prayers. # G008 Translation

Status: translated_from_pg_ocr_needs_gautier_collation

## Metadata

- Gautier ID: G008
- Recipient: the sebastos John, brother-in-law of the emperor
- Old numbering: Lami-Finetti IV; also Finetti II in the pre-Meursius series
- Source: PG 126, public OCR packet PG096; aligned by GIBI index, p. 87

## English Translation

Who would give me leisure for words, my most venerable protector, as much leisure as I desire, so that I might fit my tongue to the glory of your achievement? Who would assemble for me a chorus of rhetoricians, so that, becoming their leader as once I was, I might give them the signal and together with them complete the dance of speech?

For you have shown yourself a helper in the afflictions that have found us so severely, sent as a defender by the One who sends help from his holy place. You have crushed the heads of the dragons upon the waters; you have shattered the head of the dragon. Even if it now writhes with its tail and seems alive, in the judgment of those who judge rightly it has been broken. As for us, through you, who received power to tread upon all the power of the enemy, we have taken back life, and we laugh at the one who boasted beyond measure and said: "With my hand I shall seize the inhabited world; I shall wipe away the sea; and I shall carry off all people as though they were abandoned eggs."

But you, Lord, have not abandoned those who seek you. You looked down from heaven and saw the sons of those put to death; you had pity on us. Upon the son of man whom you strengthened for yourself, upon the man of your right hand, you sent forth your light and your truth; you increased your majesty upon the sebastos; you taught his hands for battle and his fingers for war; you shattered the arms of the sinner and broke iron bars. You brought out those who were bound and said to the lawless, "Do not act lawlessly," and to those who provoke, "Do not lift up the horn."

You see, most venerable lord, how I have hymned the gifts of God concerning you as far as I could in the words of God. For it is not fitting to diminish things beyond human measure by mere human language. Therefore preserve in goodness your imitation of God. Never wish evil to anyone. Show goodness toward all, and especially toward those who were first shown mercy by you, such as we are. You would grant us the greatest favor if you would deem our kinsmen in Euripos worthy of mercy and show those in Hellas that, in the eyes of you, the most venerable protector of all, the archbishop is worthy of some considerable regard.

Fulfill my joy, then, by taking up all the matters concerning my people. And may God exalt you still more, glorifying you because through your goodness you glorify him each day; and may your enemies lick the dust.

## Translator's Notes

- The letter is a thanksgiving and petition after a rescue from oppression. Biblical diction dominates the center of the letter.
- Several OCR intrusions between paragraphs have been omitted where they are visibly page noise rather than text.

# G009 Translation

Status: translated_from_pg_ocr_needs_gautier_collation

## Metadata

- Gautier ID: G009
- Recipient: the Caesar
- Old numbering: Lami-Finetti V
- Source: PG 126, public OCR packet PG097; aligned by GIBI index, p. 88

## English Translation

My holy lord: many times I have weighed my tongue against the good things formerly done for me by your majesty, trying to find language equal to the facts. After the attempt I found myself guilty of great lightness. The things themselves are great and weighty, while my tongue is small and has nothing weighty in it.

But now the command concerning the canonical payment has been added to those earlier gifts. This is that old Ossa upon Pelion, the monstrous thing imagined by the giants; or rather, to speak better, it is you increasing your majesty toward me. If I was already defeated by the former benefits even before this one was added, where shall I hide now? What word will there be for me, one that can somehow balance so many blessings?

Do you see how you conquer me, who once was a sharp rhetorician, most generous and kindest ruler? Do you see how you reign over us and prevail in everything? And rightly so. For if every emperor is an image of God, and you share in this most divine thing both in reality and in name, then just as the archetype is above all, so necessarily the copy too will surpass the rest.

Strike, then, in this way; draw the bow; succeed; reign by pouring out goodness. Yet I seem to hear your sweet voice answering: "What would you say, archbishop, if we were to order the remaining districts too, I mean the imperial estates, to pay you the customary canonical due?"

What else could I say except that my lord is determined to conquer not only my tongue, but also the tongue of everyone who hears his goodness proclaimed? He is like the noonday sun shining upon us, always warmer and brighter. What is better than this, holy lord: to defeat not only my tongue, but the tongue of everyone who breathes rhetoric? Shall I say something greater still? I have found God greater in the matter of ecclesiastical offerings, commanding all to complete the two-drachma payment of their subjection, and preparing rewards for goodness both here and there. Him alone you will not be able to surpass, whatever devices you contrive. Rather, the more you conquer us, the more you will be conquered by him, or rather conquered infinitely more by his acts of generosity.

## Translator's Notes

- The "canonical payment" is left general until the fiscal/ecclesiastical details can be checked against Gautier and the historical commentary.
- The close turns praise into theology: imperial benefaction is real, but God's recompense exceeds it without measure.

# G010 Translation

Status: translated_from_pg_ocr_needs_gautier_collation

## Metadata

- Gautier ID: G010
- Recipient: the son of the sebastokrator
- Old numbering: Lami-Finetti VI
- Source: PG 126, public OCR packet PG098; aligned by GIBI index, p. 90

## English Translation

Blessed be God, who has given us all the other honors of his outpouring goodness, and especially letters: through them friends greet friends, and servants address their masters from far away.

So now I too, the servant of your goodness, have used the letter as my mouth, and through it I bring the voice that is owed. First, it is a prayer for your health, my most venerable protector; and after the first and earlier prayers, it is also this: help me, my helper, wall and rampart together, to call you in the words of Scripture. Then it is an exhortation toward the good.

Do not forget, I beg you, either your own nature, from which streams of good things flow for you, or your father's counsels, than which nothing is at once more intelligent and more stable. Know that doing good belongs to God, while doing evil is a thing of the avenging Furies. Rise up, therefore, on behalf of the misery of the poor and the groaning of the needy, and against those who bring these sufferings upon them. Do not grow weary, and do not consider anything more precious than imitation of God.

May the God of the poor, or rather the God whose poor they are, keep you above every evil and injury in all things; and may he keep you taking up, by your power and height, the cause of my weakness and lowliness.

## Translator's Notes

- The opening explicitly reflects on letters as a substitute mouth across distance, a recurring motif in Theophylact's correspondence.
- "Wall and rampart" renders the doubled protective image in the Greek.

# G011 Translation

Status: translated_from_pg_ocr_needs_gautier_collation

## Metadata

- Gautier ID: G011
- Recipient: the sebastos John, son of the sebastokrator
- Old numbering: Lami-Finetti VII
- Source: PG 126, public OCR packet PG099; aligned by GIBI index, p. 90

## English Translation

Since God has sent your protection to the poor, the members of God, my most venerable lord and protector, do not cease doing the thing for which you came out. In the words of the divine Arsenios, I say to you, who are most divine in nature and in education: in all other matters too, help the poor against those who take them captive, for that is the more fitting way to speak; and drive away this evil fate of theirs as well.

One of the monks and priests, a man from among my troubles, has despised God and become shameless even before human reproach. He has taken upon himself that prophetic phrase, the "face of a prostitute." He has cast off the holy habit. A glutton instead of a faster, licentious instead of self-controlled, or rather completely false in self-control, he lives with a woman and parades his debauchery without shame.

This is worthy of many tears, but it is still more bearable than what must now be said. Listen, then, to the thing that especially provokes God and grieves us, you who alone are able to correct it. Since he has shaken off divine fear and said to God, "I do not wish to know your ways," he longs and hastens to be powerful in wickedness, and becomes for the men of Iasippos the agent of unjust and unlawful exactions.

They cherish him. Why would they not? They all but carry him on their heads. The poor, meanwhile, are carried headlong and destroyed by false accusations, with no one to ransom or save them.

I sent for the accursed man and brought him to me. He gave me security that until the Lord's Days of the meat-fast no party of his choosing would be found or bring an accusation against anyone. I released him; and the Christians, the men of Iasippos, are cherishing him and keeping him as their hunter, for that is what he calls himself, offering to them the prey they desire.

Rise up over these matters, my lord. Speak the word, and the demon that harasses the people of God will be driven away. Let no one deceive you with empty words, saying that the thief will repent. How many medicines of repentance I brought to him last year and this year, both by letter and in person when I met him! But I was speaking to a deaf wave, and the leopard returned again to its spots.

Have mercy, then, on the poor, and before them on that man's soul, which is corrupted by wickedness before he corrupts others by his injuries. Let this pest and public disease be driven away from these members. If I myself am able to seize him, he will die in the tower as a common and public evil. May God preserve my holy lord above every evil and injury.

## Translator's Notes

- The language is intentionally severe. Theophylact frames a corrupt cleric as both moral scandal and instrument of fiscal/political oppression.
- The phrase "face of a prostitute" echoes prophetic invective; it should not be flattened into mere "shamelessness."

# G012 Translation

Status: translated_from_pg_ocr_needs_gautier_collation

## Metadata

- Gautier ID: G012
- Recipient: the same addressee as G011
- Old numbering: Lami-Finetti VIII
- Source: PG 126, public OCR packet PG100; aligned by GIBI index, p. 92

## English Translation

The gifts of divine men, my most venerable lord and protector, ought to be without regret, just as God's gifts are. Since, then, your eminence first granted to us, from the God-crowned and mighty emperor, and bestowed upon the Church a kind of divinity of authority, if I may speak so - I mean the complete authority over the priests of the neighboring towns, burdened only by the tax of the zeugnelion and set above all other acts of molestation - why should you now change so great a grace, my holy lord and protector?

Even if we had moved in some way against your glory and had stained the good already done through ingratitude, even then one ought not overturn a favor done to the house of God; for the Church is both called and believed to be God's house. But if we inscribe you as lord and benefactor, for we have not become so mad as to prove ungrateful and respond perversely to your kindness, you see how easily the sigillion of the most venerable Komnenos has been overturned by Komnenos himself. That touches, in some small way, both reproach and boldness.

Yet it is not impossible to place a salve upon the wound. Indeed the salve is easier than the blow: a heavenly salve, signed beneath and defined by your venerable hand for the representative official, that the priests should be released from every molestation and servitude belonging to the praetorium in regard to lay exactions, and that whatever has been taken from priests, clerics, or any others should be returned.

I must experience a good reversal, or rather one ordered in person through the great protection of my lord: if any of the men said to be intermediaries have priests and misuse them for their private services, they should no longer possess this tyranny over the archbishops subject to them. For these men have also stirred up the common people to accuse me before my lord because of the priests, wishing to harvest the priests for themselves and breaking the privilege of the Church for their own gains.

May Jesus Christ, our first high priest, keep my most venerable lord in every sound condition of body and spirit. And do not disdain to receive a small blessing, itself complete in number: one hundred salted fish, [a final phrase in the PG/OCR is damaged].

## Translator's Notes

- This letter concerns ecclesiastical privilege and fiscal burden. I have left `zeugnelion` transliterated pending historical annotation.
- The closing gift of salted fish is secure; the sentence after it is too damaged in the PG/OCR to translate confidently.

# G013 Translation

Status: translated_from_pg_ocr_needs_gautier_collation

## Metadata

- Gautier ID: G013
- Recipient: Caesar Melissenos
- Old numbering: Lami-Finetti IX
- Source: PG 126, public OCR packet PG101; aligned by GIBI index, p. 93

But if you intend to spend time there because of him, so that no financial injury may happen to affairs from an accounting conducted in your absence, much more ought you to have spent the time in assembly with us and in the administration of ecclesiastical matters, where the benefit is more divine and the harm more dangerous.

Nothing so trips itself up as a mouth that has learned to say what is not, and tries to cure one falsehood by another. Since, then, we have determined, brother, that you deliberately postponed the assembly and sought and invented excuses - for that saying of Solomon is truly accurate, that a man who wishes to separate from friends seeks excuses - and since your opinion makes these things clear, composed as it was, unless some malice stood between, simply and with no artifice, I impose on you, in the Holy Spirit, the penalty of suspension from liturgy. You have become debtor to all for completing the judgment begun in the assembly.

For you must necessarily choose one of two things: either you did not then begin it according to the Spirit, or now you are withdrawing from it unspiritually. This too is surely clear: if those who turned toward Orthodoxy have indeed turned, you have by now performed the customary rites for them. If you have already performed them, you should have persuaded them to endure this harsh interval until your coming to us and your release from us. Those who were persuaded by you to accept the mystery, if indeed they were persuaded, would quickly have yielded to you in this as well. This too, if it had happened, would have been a test of the genuineness of the gift, and would have stopped the mouths of many who can say that the Armenians did not make their approach because they recognized the truth, as you openly declared. Recognition of the truth, among its other effects, disposes those who recognize it to endure.

Nevertheless, we yield this point too to you and allow you, after receipt of this letter, to be free for fifteen days. During all these days those who have come over will presumably be chrismated. After that you will have the yoke of suspension from liturgy laid upon your neck. If it seems harsh to you that the church under you should be deprived of your hierarchical ministry, consider that it is harsh for other churches too, because of your absence, to be deprived of bishops. Know well that if you yourself are not present, neither will the judgment concerning Lipenion receive its completion, nor will the other widowed churches receive bishops. Otherwise the tongue that strikes in secret will again have room to send cowardly and ignoble slanders against opponents, and to shoot in the dark at the upright in heart.

Since one of the Bulgarian monks, while we were sitting in council, also lamented before us about an old man, his brother, a monk and superior of the monastery in the Kastron of Saint John, saying that he has been confined and enjoys no consolation whatever from anyone, because your reverence has forbidden everyone by proclamation even to approach this monk at all - if these things are true, they are certainly unworthy of hierarchical humanity and necessarily worthy of swift correction. Let correction proceed from your honor, while the Lord keeps you showing forth in all goodness and truth the fruit of righteousness of the Spirit.

- Old numbering: Finetti XIX

## English Translation

What seemed good to the assembled brethren when your letter was read - the letter in which you excused yourself from coming here - has been made clear to you in another letter. Now hear what I have to say privately, after reading that most wise defense which you composed in reply to my letter, the one that dealt with the expulsion of the monk and the withholding of liturgical service.

It seems to me, brother, either that you did not read that letter, or that, though you read it, your mind was occupied with other, more necessary and loftier things, and so you did not understand the writers' meaning at all. How could writings that, as I think, display complete gentleness be full of anger and bitterness? If you wish, bring out the letter and have it read to people capable of understanding. You will certainly be caught either not having understood it yourself or having made it your business to slander us in everything. Indeed, what will you not say where refutation is not easy, if you lay a calumnious tongue even upon things so plainly blameless?

As for "bad company corrupts good morals," it was fitting for Paul to say this to the Corinthians. It is not fitting for you to say it to us; perhaps it is not even reverent or holy. Either you lie in calling us fathers, and it is not unclear to which father you have adopted yourself - the father of falsehood, with whom God will destroy all who speak lies - or you speak truthfully, and then, by reviling your fathers, you are worthy of the punishment assigned in the law to those who strike their fathers.

How have we given our ears to the monk, when we were so far from immediately making him trustworthy that we even stopped, from the Gospel, the mouths of those urging the proper inquiry? "Does our law judge a man," it says, "unless it first hears him and knows what he is doing?" And how have we condemned you in your absence, when we summoned you to be present at the synod and make your defense? To call the accused to a defense is not, I think, to condemn him. Nor have I learned, down to the present day, that restoring someone who possesses something, and had been driven out of that possession, and then giving room for the tribunal concerning ownership, is condemnation.

But if, as one who ordains new laws, you require us to relearn these things, you will first be required by us to ennoble your tongue with more justice, so that you may not cause gurgling laughter among educated youths whenever they read through your barbarous letters; then you may become a lawgiver and teacher for us.

It seems to me that you did not understand at all the imperial documents that the old man carried. Otherwise you would not have written to us that it seemed good to our mighty and holy emperor that the monk should not have his own cell. The revered memorandum plainly contains the opposite. As for what your innocent heart attached to the official in charge of petitions - that, when the monk tyrannized by you gave him a litra of Michaelata, he composed legal claims contrary to the emperor's decision, which is the same as fabricating - another man, hearing directly the judgment of the great Gregory, would say that the bad man would most readily condemn even the good, while the good man would not easily condemn even the bad.

I, however, know that the official in charge of petitions is most closely attached to the emperor, for no other reason than that he aims at the imperial will and never shoots beside that target. I would sooner be persuaded that you have once said something as it really is, something that exists and truly happened, than that this man fabricated something that did not happen; for he knows that such a thing does not please our true emperor, established by the true God and King of all. But let this matter stand with you as you wish. Certainly he alone has not had so great a good fortune as to escape the tongue of blame, which touches even the best men when lodged in the worst ears.

Since you are always accustomed to slander us, you were ashamed now to abandon the habit. Using it again, you accused us in your letter, saying that we ordered you to renounce your episcopal right over the monastery. This is plainly a patchwork of the kind you have practiced stitching together. We did not ask you to stand aside from a just episcopal right, but from an unjust assault. By unjust assault I mean one not confirmed by judgment but strengthened by tyranny, swollen to such a point that even now it has no restraint, but brings more and heavier injuries on the monk confined and cut off from every bodily consolation - if the things that his brother lamented before the bishops, in the hearing also of one subject to you, are not wholly remote from the truth. For what your meekness has now decreed against the wretched old man is plainly a prohibition of fire and water, the penalty established by the ancient laws for those who commit the last of all crimes.

How could I willingly pass over without refutation the final section of your letter? You said that since we are angry with you, you absolutely must not appear at the synod, lest you become the cause of greater scandal. But, best of fellow bishops, this very thing is what makes us angry: that you do not yield, among other matters, even when summoned to the synod. Thus the scandal is multiplied against you. You are unaware that you are doing something like a feverish man drinking wine who, seeing the physician angry, swears that he will never take water, as if by that [...]

What madness, what delusion possessed you, pitiable man, that you dared to write such things to a man who spares your weakness and covers it over? If I chose to let my tongue loose against your stupidity and ignorance in everything, you would descend into the recesses of Hades while still alive. I speak as one deranged; you forced me to it. I marveled also at this piece of your malice: how you made no mention at all of the bond of suspension from liturgy that the monk said was laid on him by you. You knew what consequence follows from this, and therefore you passed it over in silence.

So we too add nothing now beyond what seemed good to us about this in the former letter, since those measures were canonical and above every accusation. I have written these things to you contrary to my accustomed manner, seeing that meekness has inflamed your madness of pride. Since meekness proved useless, I have now moderately pushed it aside and shown a dim fruit of fire. May it be granted to you to recognize your own measure, as the Lord gives you understanding in all things, and may he press down and melt away this mercilessness of yours, which we see written largely in you, changing the savagery and brutality by which an old priest-monk is suffering evil into meekness and sympathy toward a monk, a hierarch, and now an old man.

## English Translation

Neither I nor the circumstances in which we have been have had much leisure. If leisure were with us, the matter might perhaps have been painful, since something possible was not happening. But since neither is possible, it is truly foolish to seek what cannot be done. Let us will the other thing, I mean that he, though cut off from us, may enjoy relief.

## English Translation

## English Translation

Indeed, why should you not receive every unpleasant thing pleasantly because of your greatness of soul, since you even judge my words, which are more unpleasant than almost anything, sweeter than all things? You, the eagle, confess yourself charmed by the croaking of the crow; and you believe that the fish from our parts, made pleasant in the same way by my words, can rouse an appetite deadened to everything else.

You speak in a way worthy of your goodness, lifting yourself higher by the very things through which, in condescension, you raise up my lowliness. ## English Translation

I had already become dead, most august and greatest helper of mine, and had been set in the lowest places of Hades. Now I am raised by you. Restoring my little soul, you gather my bones, fit together what had fallen apart, grow flesh over it, stretch skin around it, and become for me a giver of life.

One would not be wrong to call that hope the breath of a soul fixed in calamities. All my reasonings were dissolved, and I lay dead in despair of any correction of the evil.

But when your most noble son-in-law, the shoot of goodwill, the lily in the valleys of this present flood of evils, the rose among the thorns that prick everyone, a truly God-breathed soul, came over the affairs near us, he appeared to me as a strange sight: a sweet river flowing through brine, a traveler passing through mud without being stained. I recognized, surely, the effects of your commands. Then the winged breath of better hope flew back into me, and everything took place around me that belongs to Ezekiel's fearful vision.

Rather, he became fragrant to God through the poor, God smelling the scent of his works more sweetly than the sacrifice of Noah of old. Then he passed over into your air, leaving us only the memory, without consolation. Who from now on will gather abundance for those who own no land, and lead those submerged in the drink of poverty into the order of plenty? Who will give orphans almost reason to confess gratitude to death, because by being deprived of weak fathers they gained the man who then appeared to them? Whose commands, echoes of divine goodness, will set a boundary firm for widows?

Whose eyes, shedding tears for the wronged, will mourn still more for those who do wrong, since they are diminished in greater things, and will hasten to undo the losses for both sides - the greater loss for one, the lesser for the other? Whom will the poor find as avenger against rapacious rich men, and the rich as advocate against malicious and slanderous poor men? Whom will the order of God's servants find as an attendant who does not make discriminations among ranks, the thing pursued by the godless, who in dishonoring God's servants furnish themselves with an apology against those entitled to honor by alleging their unworthiness?

Rather, he looked at all of them as better than himself; he believed that through them he was sanctified by the anointing of the Spirit, and that divine and sanctifying rites were still being performed. Or rather, to speak more truly, he did not pain even them. They judged the young man's magnificence in such works to be greater than all profit, an acquisition of wealth and not a loss. This is the mark of good servants: to choose poverty that enriches their master with good report rather than wealth that impoverishes him through ill report.

But for that noble and good sebastos the place of office was something to flee, truly a place of corruption, a tent of Kedar, an iron furnace. For this reason he flies almost winged to your blessedness. Those who enjoyed his kindness lament themselves, since they will be tested by hardships more heavily than before - evil is worse after one has had experience of the good. Yet they will pray from the Lord every outpouring of good for you and yours, because you teach in this way and they are taught in this way. I am not ashamed to be ranked first among them. May God grant to you yourselves, to your household, and to those around you completion in the good, a good flow of prosperity, keeping you utterly above every corruption and injury.

# G081 Translation

Status: translated_from_pg_ocr_needs_gautier_collation

## Metadata

- Gautier ID: G081
- Recipient: Gregory Taronites
- Old numbering: Meursius XXVI
- Source: PG 126, public OCR packet PG046; aligned by Gautier/Mullett concordance

## English Translation

From where, and with what thanks, could I repay you, my most magnificently distinguished son and lord in the Lord, since you show me every time to be the truest of all men? The praises with which I seem to ennoble you, you yourself ennoble still more by demanding them in return - not as sophistic flatteries of a rhetorician, but as leaps of the Spirit rejoicing in things truly noble, and as songs from a mouth that knows how to bless virtue.

For behold what was reported to me a little while ago: how highly truth-loving and unenvious ears would prize it. By a single trophy you lowered the brows of two nations, and with the tower of Persian madness you pulled down the height of Frankish madness as well. You appeared to us as a new Phinehas, piercing together with your mighty spear two nations unclean before the Lord.

The man who used to tax Nesman and the other Greek cities around the Pontus within the Tanais River and Lake Maeotis - including the chief city of the Colchians, bounded by the Phasis, and the city set high over Little Armenia, indeed all Armenia, to say nothing of the Mariandynians, Galatians, and neighboring Cappadocians - had grown fat and heavy from tax-levies. His tax-collecting hands have now been cut off by the edge of the sword, and he has learned instead to be taxed. His raids, with which he had become bold in plundering, were checked; he abandoned the ambushes that were more obvious to Taronites than a city set on a mountain. He recognized himself as dry-handed, or rather handless, for pitched battles; and cities of ten thousand men which he had captured with difficulty he lost more quickly than speech can tell. Thus the die of fortune has fallen for him into the heaviest misfortune. Rather, the scales of God tipped toward him the pan of your virtues, heavier than the deeds of those on whom he feasted before your command.

From this he has become convinced that the ancient trophies sung of the Romans were no myth - indeed, that what is said about them falls short of the truth. In you he sees the truth. I will even say that he thinks you surpass those men by as much as you, setting out from more deficient preparations, match yourself against them; so mighty is the impulse you give to what affairs lack. He wishes you had never been born, yet admires you as a noble man born. He curses your birth and calls those who begot you blessed. He prays childlessness for you, so that your line may be enclosed in you, but for himself he prays for the births of such children. And now, after being lifted so high by former good fortunes, he understands by experience that he was lifted so that he might suffer more when deprived of more. He has only one consolation: that he has suffered so much at the hands of the bravest of Romans, for the defeated also love to be glorified by the greatness of those who defeat them. If Homer were present, he would now have said of you also that a noble man fled, and a greater man pursued him.

Be hopeful too, fairest of cities, Neocaesarea, that before long you will have Gregory as your liberator. You are not unused to being benefitted by men named Gregory; and once you have scraped away the old age of your present misery, you will truly appear as New-Caesarea. You have pledges in your neighbors. Pray for the noble bridegroom who will be joined to you by victory over you and crowned with a crown of precious stone; for Christ sets up the trophies for Taronites.

The godless Turk now, because of you, bends downward and sees only what lies at his feet - he who yesterday swept the arrogant pupils of his eyes everywhere and planned to wipe out land and sea together. Therefore he now looks to the terms of agreement as his only helpers of salvation. He stretches out his hands no longer to draw the sword, but to give pledges; laying aside the bow, he takes up the herald's staff, and he desires to have as a firm friend the man whom he found unconquerable as an enemy.

The Frank, whose neck was iron until now but proved softer than warm wax, bows to you in his abundance, and through you to our mighty emperor. And why should he not, when he sees the one he himself had worshipped now worshipping, and the one who bound others held in bonds more unbreakable by the will of Taronites? He is so mastered by these bonds that he grants to the emperors his own ransom, though he once pursued it and counted it an affair worth everything to possess as a purchased slave the man who made himself liberator of all the East. He wandered in such long dreams that he would not tolerate even second place in imperial honor; but you alone have prospered as the agent of the thing pursued. For the Turk, compelled by your hands, unwillingly agreed to truces and to other terms, including the surrender of the Frank to our unconquered emperor.

For these things I owe you thanks in another way as well. As has been said, it is not only that I am shown truthful by you, but also that those who bear the name of Christ in common, and the Roman rule, receive good counsel and the help of prayer. As for our all-good emperor, what need is there to say how worthily he will repay your wise counsel and courage? Always preferring you before all - for what emperor has ever been more skilled than he in judging men? - he gives you to affairs most in need of active oversight, like a most skilled physician set against terrible diseases and a helmsman set against a storm.

But what a thing a letter is! It is truly a bridle holding back a rhetorical mouth and refusing to allow it to revel at greater length in noble deeds. By it I too am reined back from eagerness to say more. If free speech were available to me, I would run verbal races for you, not falling short of your chariot of virtues so far as words can say - for that chariot is driven for you with wings. But since what comes from me is sufficient, and perhaps not worthy to be hissed at in the theater of speech, I shall say only this and then stop.

Lift your eyes to the mountains, from where your help has come, and will come again and again. Do not cast them downward; do not turn them toward yourself. Things so great do not belong to human strength, either alone or chiefly, but wholly - or at least mostly - to divine grace. In God you do power, the God who crushes enemies. You will confirm this grace in yourself, and draw still more of it, by being grateful to the one who grants grace. You would become the image of him if you are conformed to him through goodness, poured out upon rich and poor, upon those who are sacred in the stricter sense and those who are commoner - or, to speak better, upon the more divine and the more human - insofar as each has need of it. Through this goodness may you be delivered from evils and from the Evil One, so that you neither suffer harm nor become corrupted.

- The "Frank" and "Turk" passages require historical collation, but the rhetorical sense is clear.

# G085 Translation

Status: translated_from_pg_ocr_needs_gautier_collation

## Metadata

## English Translation

Who am I, my lord, lord, that you have loved me so far that I may now utter to you what David once uttered to God? "Why have you laid so great a burden on me, when all my strength for carrying burdens is broken?" You command me, as though I were the very pillars that hold up the broad heaven and as though I needed the strength of Atlas, to part the clouds of the prophetic writing and show the sun bare.

But who will blow away for me the cloud of affairs which the reports of slanderers have thickened over our honored sun? Whoever this sun may be, he is beautiful as a bridegroom, huge as a giant, giving life to all by his heat and warmth and conquering all. He ought to melt these clouds, so that even if they arise they do not appear; yet he thickens them into a formation of clouds - perhaps reasonably, but, as it seems to most, contrary to reason. Before we have clear weather from the former things, Eurus and Notus have fallen together again, and a bitter Zephyr, and night rushes down from heaven. This drives more and sharper goads of despondency into me, whenever I have darkness from above, from the place where I had hoped for light.

But lest I too make clouds by obscurity and shade the sun of speech, here is the matter. The unjust witnesses who rose against me yesterday and the day before - whom our mighty emperor, your much-loved brother, exposed in their falsehood and made fall from their plots, so that injustice itself was proved false - have now come to him again, saying the same things about the same matters. They have gained such power that they persuaded him to throw into the void those white votes which he had granted us as just, and to bring out black ones in their place, full of much gloom. That truly imperial judgment has been made to look like Penelope's web, woven by the day of truth and undone by the night of falsehood.

Most dreadful of all, we are being condemned in absence. As I have learned, the registrar sent against us has been ordered to remove from the Church the estate in Ohrid - an estate for which documents of exchange had already been issued, the Church having received it in place of the wandering properties which she ceded to the public treasury because of paroikic service. The matter was more necessary than everything else, and a chrysobull was added to establish the exchange. An imperial note had even threatened those Cercopes when they were pestering Heracles.

In these circumstances, what kind of soul do you think I have? How could I fail, with the divine Jeremiah, to feel pain in my belly and to have my senses rage, thrown into confusion from every side and almost driven to frenzy? The noble Thucydides says that what is sudden, unexpected, and contrary to reason enslaves the mind. Even by yesterday's slanders, though I was not firmly rooted out, I was bent down simply because such a thing had been sown in the good soil of the imperial hearing like hemlock, aconite, or some other poisonous fig.

By those slanders, then, I was bent down in my misery so that I stooped to the ground and inclined downward, having no official support, struck by slaves and forced to bear it. O Christ, who reigns in the palaces, and who did not even when judged and slapped cease to bear mildly a blow from a slave: whom do you suppose would hesitate to lay hands on us when he sees our accusers passing by like demigods on mules, as people say, making a drunken tale of this, that they fabricated this and that against a high priest before the most pious emperors, escaped judgment, and then laughed over it?

Here too David's word has been fulfilled in me: those who sit in the gates babbled against me, and those who drink wine made songs about me. Surely they will tolerate me very well if I rebuke, correct, or exhort them, whether they belong to the people or to the ecclesiastical body, when they have seen someone acting worse than anyone after the previous reproofs. Because those subject to those words became savage, they surrounded me with words of hatred and fought me without cause. Over all this, not only did they pay no penalty, but they shine in wickedness as though with a trophy; fear is nowhere. They remain like sheep forever and complete their life in good things. It is not untimely to speak with long-suffering Job.

I used to hear of the Lernaean Hydra, which became twice itself at each cutting and gave trouble to noble Heracles when he raised only a sword against it. The hero would have been in danger - or the labor would certainly have been too much even for the hero - if he had not added good Iolaus as a fire-bearing comrade to burn the cuts. What does the myth mean? It seems to me that wickedness - for that is the Hydra - does not need a superficial cutting and punishment. We would make the wicked more wicked if they were punished so mildly, since they would think they had mocked justice and would from then on speak their wickedness more fearlessly and nakedly. Boldness is multiplied in evil people when they experience a penalty milder than the severe one they expected. Therefore the outgrowths of evil must not only be cut off; the springs of the blood that produces and feeds the beastly heads must also be dried up. If evil shoots are not cut even in this simple way, then, as the proverb says, it is a Lerna of evils.

Even at the former outrages my heart abandoned me - by which I mean the boiling of spirit I used against every malice, when I showed my tongue truly a pupil of lordly tongues. If I now receive the blows from the man presently making the register, who, as they say, has received a heavy scourge from imperial hands and been ordered to use Isaiah's words against us in alien fashion - for this is what those who hate the reprover in the gates and abhor a holy word have accomplished, as they threaten - then let Baruch stand by me here as secretary to Jeremiah while he laments, preserving my calamities as songs for the future.

After such stains, and so many of them, have been splashed upon us, do you think the mirror of my mind can receive divine images, when even the light of my eyes is no longer with me? This is both likely and reasonable. Nevertheless, since we have entrusted our affairs to God, to the one who reigns from him and through him, and to the goodness of your Greatness, we are confident that our hope will not be put to shame, that the endurance of the poor will not perish in the end, and that wickedness will not overpower wisdom, if one must obey what wisdom itself suggests.

When we have become a little easier, we shall take up the work commanded and send a taste to you, who are most critical in taste. Others whom he found in the city, needing the emperor for other reasons, he persuaded to join the outcry, so as to defame my name. Recently he claimed that he had been burned out by me, although he had not been burned out at all by anyone and had nothing left to lose. Since he does no work, but meddles; since he has made it his craft to attach himself to the tax officials and, like dogs at tables and messmates, to look only for whatever may be thrown him by them, barking first and over-barking later, he is barer than a crab. Even if he had been burned out, it would have nothing to do with me, no more than events in Libya have to do with the Indians at Nysa. I was away from Ohrid when he says he suffered that dreadful non-event; in another way I was staying in Pelagonia, held down by serious illness.

What obscure thing would he not say and be believed with pledges, when he marches openly against things so plain? Though the emperor is a very well-sealed man and not easily given to false tales, the utterly wicked fellow softened him all the same. What posture did he not assume, what did he not lament, what oath did he spare, until he had magnified the burning and persuaded him that he had suffered these things from the archbishop as retribution for the goodwill which that man had sheared away?

He even swore that roads pour with cheese for me and that my mountains pour with milk; that I am rich and deep in wealth through the accumulation of I do not know how many talents; that I live like a satrap - indeed, that Median splendor, compared with archiepiscopal splendor, would appear paltry, and the royal halls at Susa and Ecbatana mere huts beside my many-storied and ventilated houses, in which I spend the summer cooling the furnace of the flesh that appears upon me. All this is a fabrication of a demonic judgment and a phantom of a tongue that has learned only to speak evil. Apart from the much-discussed land, I, mighty in strength and rival of the Aloadae, have not been found holding any public property, although I have been searched and examined maliciously.

Let no one condemn me in absence by immediately accepting the tax official's slanders against me as trustworthy. Whatever he says, let him also wait for the defenses from the side of the Church. This is what I especially ask of your Majesty. I ask nothing new, nothing unusual to the holy and most divine soul of the emperor, but only that what he preserves in every case and at all times be preserved in my case too: that he not be preempted by the words of an enemy and avenger.

If the man says that he can bring gains to the treasury from the clerics, "Hippocleides does not care." I am not accountable for this. First, they were not previously profited from us; each man enjoyed exemption for one yoke-team, and beyond that they paid the public treasury. Second, before this they had exemption from the tithe on all animals. No grateful judge would call this theft from the public treasury, but an undefined imperial generosity enjoyed abundantly by those who found it. Now, with that generosity receiving a limit and a known fixation, what lies beyond the definition has reasonably been added to the public treasury and has increased the weight of the demand.

Third - and this is worthy of tears - most of them did not enjoy even the exemption for one yoke-team or the defined exemption on animals. Because the man is more a condemnatory register-maker than a registrar, to say something ridiculous, he added yoke-teams and animals to them, and they were not only demanded payment on things for which they were entitled to remain exempt, but over-demanded. They suffered the same lawless treatment in other headings too. On mills the clerics paid double what laymen paid; and on the things called strougai in the Bulgarian language, which a Greek would call channels, used for catching fish, they too were fined many times more than the common multitude.

The "faithful and pious" man contrived these things so that the gains brought to the public treasury from the clerics might be swollen, and so that I might be slandered as ungrateful and greedy, preserving so great a loss for my benefactor and helper. I say the same about the taxes he demanded from the people of the Church. These too were demanded most unjustly: monks, consecrated eunuchs, and even a few laymen, not more than five, I think - all of them receiving nothing from the public treasury. How could they be judged fit to be taxed? Not even an emperor can do this.

He collected from me personally in such abundance that he did not dishonor the magnitude of my rank. For mills long since demolished he demanded everything; for those in existence he demanded twice as much as from the Bulgarians. For a small marshy spot that gave a poor catch of fish, he forced me to pay thirteen units, though I took from there fish worth no more than a single diobol, while everyone else paid one diobol for similar places. Although the Church possessed exemption, by imperial gift, for large and small animals, the men just mentioned were tithed on the few animals they had, which I had given them as a small consolation.

To pass over most things, lest I be counted the proverbial Arab flute-player, he made useless in every respect the order recently issued by your goodness. Regarding the estate, he frightened the villagers in countless ways, saying he would impose taxes on each of them beyond their capacity if they should choose the archbishopric again as master. He committed another wrong by not handing over to the Church the surplus persons in the estate, for whom he is obliged to carry out such an imperial order. When we asked that these people be registered according to each person's means and then handed over to us, he grew savage and, taking offense at our request alone, said: "Even if I ask that the first be paid by the Church as the seventh at the time of completion, you will pay so much unwillingly. Who has resisted my will? Who will judge me if I value what is worth so much at this much?" Yet I know the wisest Judith applied to God alone the saying, "You spoke, and they came to be; you thought, and they stood present." But this man makes himself a partner in that blessedness - who is he, what is he, how great is he, how mighty, beneath whom they will spread decay?

He adds this too: even in the case of the paroikoi given to the Church, whom he very unjustly registered and increased, he will grant the Church nothing except the single yoke-team, and he threatens to retain all the other headings himself. The order concerning the handing over of the estate plainly does not intend this. He does these things so that, disgusted by them, I may become more inactive about the consolation of the estate, and from there his most charming companion Lazaros may become master of these people.

In handing over the land, he shortens the measure of the rope and also behaves with utter ignorance in the calculation of modioi. How could he know the principles of geometry? Why do I register the number of sand grains and drops of rain? In every way, place, cause, time, matter, and person, this most just man engineers losses and harms for me, and contrives to rub stains upon me through which he knows his own plans are most likely to succeed.

For all these things I ask, as I said, that a place for defense be kept for me too, and that his words not be received as oracles. When the shadows are dissolved, truth will tear away the lion-mask and show the ugly monkey sitting underneath. Above all, I ask that an order be issued allowing me unhindered departure or rescue from here, so that I may not be swallowed up by despondency; for my strength is not the strength of stones. May my soul be taken untimely if, in this matter at least, I have in any way acted contrary either to God or to your Majesty.

Recently he claimed that he had been burned out by me, though he had suffered no burning at all from anyone and had, simply, nothing to give as fuel to the fire or to lose, no more than drones have a honeycomb. Nor will he surrender what he has set in order through firm and unreasoned orders to those who tear it down. May God, who rescues the wronged from a hand stronger than theirs, either there or by guiding affairs, keep for you in the future the fuller rewards for our rescue.

# G127 Translation

Status: translated_from_pg_ocr_merged_packet_needs_gautier_collation

## Metadata

- Gautier ID: G127
- Recipient: Gregory Kamateros, protasekretis
- Old numbering: Meursius LXXI
- Source: PG 126, public OCR packet PG088, second unit; aligned by GIBI index

## English Translation

I am now the most blessed of all men, if through my brother and through my letter I have benefitted you, a man who has received two blessednesses. When we do good to someone very small, as the Lord says, we hope for rewards that mind cannot grasp and speech cannot utter. But if we benefit someone as great as you - made great both by the dignity of logothete, the man who of old sat with the emperor, and by the office of protasekretis, the one always serving as secretary to emperors, especially now when the emperor is wiser and more harmonious, so that he works both other things and words himself - ah, the multitude and greatness of the rewards! I am already receiving pledges of them.

If I did not know that I would chafe you by the length of the letter, when you have already been sufficiently pricked by letters and have sharply interpreted sufferings of the soul in another way, using the methods of the Stagirite, I would count out for you each kind of pledge one by one. But now I shall mention one only.

I was persuaded that the all-venerable son of the protostrator, who inherits from his father in other respects as well as in his disposition toward words - the children of my own youth - had been sent against the Vardariotes. Honoring words as they deserve, because of the zeal he had spent on them, he will not leave even us, who seem to be something in them and have fallen into his hands, without experience of his greatness. We shall enjoy such things as we shall always remember. Is this pledge small to you, or is it the principal of a most profitable investment? For my part, I cannot even contain it; it is so much wider than my narrowness.

Having gained such great things from the benefits I gave you, I will gladly benefit another too, and I will make known to you my Cyclops - the very thing you asked for through your pure and pearly letter. This fellow, besides his man-eating, always shows me one eye, never blinking at my injury: whether one tries to accommodate him more closely, or deal with him more simply, or make a joke with him; whether I bring Pramnian wine in a full bowl; whether the son of fragrant thighs gives me the speech still remaining in the little skin of my body, the drug that withers every grief - still he lays upon me one and the same eye, the sign of one and the same disposition.

Trim around this description, as though I were wearing a sacred fillet, and see who behaves this way toward me. If you find him, you will know my Polyphemus. You see, I have all but pointed him out to you with my finger. Judge from this whether I cry out in vain to my Virgin and to the Ram tied to the tree of release. I am afraid that he may be the first to host me, as he promises.

In this condition you did a most kindly work by remitting for me the charge of outrage. You did not mount again upon a man lying down; you did not mutilate Hector when he was dead. Because you refrained from this, you have the favor of sitting beside the emperor, whose goodness even his enemies witness. Therefore God repays you for your love of mankind by giving in return soul-guiding consolation: sweet Theodore, truly Chryselios, who has gathered his name from two incomparable things in the world, and who has fled what is truly laborious in us while becoming tireless himself.

How could he have been educated in so many arts and so well if his nature were laborious and collapsing? How could he have brought military science and geometry into one and joined things long separated by high walls since Archytas, Philolaus, the other Adrian, and our fallen Julian? I knew he was an arithmetician from the time he handled the Vardariotes; he counted even in the middle of the night. Some arithmetical theorem would strike him out of sleep, since he was not submerged even in dreams. He too could truly say, "I sleep, but my heart keeps watch." He was always calculating precisely how to cut the unit into parts and prove ignorant those who speak of things without measure. He showed cubes and cube-cubes, and interpreted them to those who did not know.

I did not yet know him as a geometer, though likelihood required him to measure land frequently, since it is always somehow slipping. Perhaps he might become a musician too. That would be just, since arithmetic is akin to harmonics; both concern determinate quantity. This would happen if first he undertook the exercises that supply breath to bronze reeds and learned to expand and contract them rhythmically, filling on one side and emptying on the other. Then he might handle the canons, and in general be trained on the other wind instruments and on the stretched and string-drawn ones.

Then he will move on to the much-hymned spherical science, or astrology, not only because it is geometry's sister - magnitude belongs to both - but because he has already practiced the homonymous "spherical" art, which he displayed to us on horseback. Telling fortunes to women, not at crossroads like those begging-diviners, but in a workshop, so as to dignify the subject, he will earn money from it. No treasure buried anywhere in the earth will escape him, a treasure able to fill him with treasures, like the one belonging to Atticus, father of Herodes the sophist. If ever he must display one of the many pursuits he knows, he will not be ignorant of the planet, the ruler, the lord of the horoscope, or the house-master of the moon; from these perhaps his misfortunes will be known. Once he has learned, he will avoid the harmful star and ward off the malefic. We shall have another Ptolemy, or, if you like, another Aratus, especially now that the business of knowledge has been driven by laws. Watching the courses of the sun as it drives its winged chariot, he will learn charioteering too. Thus he will be Chryselios not more by name than by nature.

If you ever ask him to display this art too, he will yoke to the chariot that mule of the same age as the ones that carried Priam to the tents of Achilles. It will run like the Harpies, not weighed down with flesh but light and well wrapped in body, especially if you set as a turning-post a heap of barley. Then it will see a new and desirable sight; perhaps its belly will run up to the jaws and teeth. If a strong wind is blowing, it will lift the mule into the air, and this charioteer too, since he is lighter than she is - unless, as usual, he has belted himself with staters.

If, then, you care to see him as a most approved charioteer, a prize fought over by the factions named after colors, and a famous topic of conversation for all horse-lovers, do not keep such a nature in the confinement of Macedonia where we are held. Release him to Larissa. Thessaly and the Pharsalian plain are more fitting for him. In their breadth he will display some of his sciences and arts and use others to help him learn. If he crosses over to us, he will make a show neither in the other arts nor in geometry. I have no desire for the display of the former, and there is truly no place for the latter with me, for all our lands have been turned into sea by the floods from above.

He will have one useful art for me, if he knows singing and choral direction. This too is a part or form of harmonics, though not pursued by everyone, except certainly by people like us. Therefore either let him not come here, or let him come as one who will sing more richly and variously in church, be leader of a choir, and mark the art by the movements of his fingers and the turns and bends of his other limbs. Test this for me first. If he makes the craftsman known to you and is judged approved, not even then let him trust the road to the Acherusian lake, my Lychnidos, unless he first makes agreement with my brother Demetrios about the wage for the songs. It shall be a merchant's half-medimnos of millet or black wheat. So much is fitting both for the one who will sing and for the one who will hear: the one deserves to receive it, and the other is well supplied to give it.

You see how far I have come from elderly reverence and dignity in order to please you and join in delighting over sweet Theodore. Both of us need things that guide the soul: I because of all my evils, and you because of the journey away that troubles you, that long one, as you called it. Do not be excessively distressed by it. You, if anyone, know that it is happening for the common benefit and that it displays the endurance and burden-bearing of our emperor. In order to frighten the enemies from nearby, he disregards that city, those palaces, and all their other enjoyments, enduring rustic hardships. He is exposed to cold and heat; in the open air he bears contrary seasons with his dearest people. At the same time, whatever regions he visits he sets in order magnificently and imperially, acting as governor of other affairs and especially of ecclesiastical ones. Fields and cities profit from these and countless other things belonging to your long absence. Therefore, unless you are self-loving but rather humane and brother-loving, you will not be distressed any longer at the absence; you will even pray that it be extended, so that more of us may receive more benefits.

## Translator's Notes

- This is a highly playful, learned letter. The "Cyclops" is a hostile figure Theophylact invites Kamateros to identify; Theodore Chryselios is treated in an extended mock-panegyric.
- Musical, mathematical, astrological, equestrian, and comic food-payment imagery all require close Gautier collation.

# G134 Translation

Status: translated_from_pg_and_gibi_needs_gautier_collation

## Metadata

- Gautier ID: G134
- Recipient: Tivanios the Armenian
- Date: [to verify in Gautier]
- Source: PG126 OCR packet PG022 and GIBI 9.2, pp. 228-229, Greek with facing Bulgarian translation

## English Translation

It is great folly to make one nature from the union: the created and the uncreated, the passible and the impassible, and all the other properties, some proper to human nature and others to the divine. For although they were united, the properties of each nature certainly remained unchanged. How, then, is there one nature, when even after the union they are preserved in their own differences?

Is not iron also united to fire? Yet it remains a heavy body, and one with a particular shape - round perhaps, or long and blade-like, or some other shape - even if it has become bright and hot from the fire.

How, then, does the human will, united to the divine will and remaining unconfused, as they themselves testify, not introduce a pair of two wills, its own and the divine? Let them tell me: did the Lord assume the natural will, that is, a human willing? Yes, certainly. But, they say, he deified this willing. So do I say. Behold, then, two things: that which deified and that which was deified.

"But they are united," he says. I say so too. If, however, when united they were confused, one might say that they became one confused thing, neither the one nor the other in any real sense. But since, when united, they preserve their own proper characteristics, how are they not two?

"But the human will," he says, "was led by the divine will; and when the divine will willed, then the human nature willed to eat or drink or perform some other natural operation." I say so too. Since, then, when the divine willing permitted, the human will then willed, do you see that in Christ there was a human will, willing those human things that the divine will permitted?

Is it not written in the Gospel that he wished to be hidden and could not? How, then, did he wish - divinely or humanly? For this reason he could not, God permitting it, so that the human will might be shown. And how was it shown if it was not in Christ essentially and unchangeably? For these things were not displayed in appearance only. May it not be.

The human will, then, was united to the divine will. Since it was united unchangeably and without confusion, a duality of wills is introduced even after the union. The union did nothing other than glorify and brighten what had formerly been dishonored and without glory. What was glorified did not step outside its own nature; rather, it was restored to its proper nature. Dishonor comes from sin, which is against nature, even if union with God is not natural to us but above nature.

So then the natural will, having been glorified and willing what seemed good to God, was not lost. Therefore, since it existed and the divine will also existed in Christ, two are certainly recognized even after the union by their own proper differences. So too in the case of iron made red-hot: fire, united to iron, has the property of burning and shining, and iron has its own weight, mass, and shape. The properties of fire and iron are not divided from each other hypostatically; but because they keep their natural differences, they show that even after the union the fire and the iron are natures.

- The passage argues against a monophysite/monothelite inference by distinguishing hypostatic union from confusion of natural properties.
