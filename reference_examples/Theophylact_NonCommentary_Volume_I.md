# I. On the Veneration of the Precious and Life-giving Cross

## 1

What then? Shall we keep silence before the wonder now set before us? Shall we pass by the Cross of God without praise and not embrace it also with the tongue? For all our members owe it their several acts of gratitude, since through it all have been saved. The fact that it has already been hymned by many is no reason why it should not also be blessed by us. Rather, since it has become for us too the procurer of every good, it should receive from us whatever honor lies within our power. Otherwise, by the same reasoning, God himself should not be glorified by us because he has been glorified from eternity.

And where then shall we place the words, “Generation after generation shall praise your works,” and, “A people yet to be created shall praise the Lord”—by which I understand the people continually coming into being—and all the other passages of this kind which proclaim an everlasting and unceasing confession of the works of God? Divine things are not exhausted by a single mouth, even if that mouth is Spirit-bearing. Let David persuade you when he says, “I declared and spoke; they multiplied beyond number.”

## 2

Nor shall poverty of speech bind our tongue, lest we prove poor also in thanksgiving and suffer a double shame: lacking not only an impressive offering but gratitude itself. That, to my mind, would be the more disgraceful thing. A man might perhaps be pardoned for the meanness of his language. Wisdom is not given to everyone, nor knowledge either, but to those to whom the Spirit gives it. But if we are entered on the rolls of ingratitude as useless and unprofitable servants, I do not know what argument could rescue us from condemnation—any more than those under the old tabernacle who failed to contribute whatever lay within their means.

I find, indeed, that the sacrifices were not prescribed in the same form or cost for everyone: for some they were more costly, for others humbler, according, I think, to what each one's hand could afford. This, then, is how I understand the matter, and I would gladly exhort everyone to proclaim the power of God according to the capacity he possesses, and to pour forth the memory of his goodness. By confessing our servitude we confess also our awareness of his benefits; and from this there comes both a greater apprehension of the Master's goodness and a more abundant outpouring of his gifts.

## 3

Since we have laid this foundation sufficiently, come, let us build upon it something worthy of the house of God and of the Church, something fitted to the cornerstone.

Scripture knows two creations for us: the bringing of what did not exist into existence, and the transformation of what exists badly into existing well—the first, the giving of being to nature; the second, the correction of free choice. Neither is without Word and Wisdom. The heavens proclaim this, established by the Word of the Lord; all things were made through the Word and fashioned in wisdom. And those who are created anew according to God are founded in Christ, the Wisdom of God: fitted together upon the Word, the firstborn of all creation, they grow into a holy temple and are completed as a dwelling-place of God in the Spirit.

Of this second creation—the shaping of character—the wise master-builders were Paul and the servants of the Word from the beginning, and after them the teachers who ordered all things in the churches in a Spirit of wisdom. No less than Bezalel arranging the furnishings of that wandering tabernacle, they showed God dwelling and walking among those who hold fast to these ordinances.

## 4

To enumerate the rest would exceed the present occasion and rise above our understanding, our tongue, and your hearing. Let us therefore say a little about the forty-day fast, and then we shall come to the veneration of the Cross, which is the subject before us.

Those wise builders of the Church perceived that the Son of God, for our sake, put on our nature and became like us so that we might become like him, stripping away everything that belongs merely to us. They perceived that he came not only to supply what we lacked and to grant us power and strength, but also to teach us how that power should be used. And so they made his fast, undertaken for our sake, ours as far as we are able. Through fasting the disorder of the flesh is bridled, its bonds are thinned, the captive mind is rescued, and the one who held it captive is stripped of his spoils and put to shame. He still pursues us, certainly, but not as before does spirit overpower flesh, for flesh weakened by fasting no longer stands in the same condition. He finds it drawn near to spirit, and therefore keeps his distance, seeing God beside the one who fasts: God, who is accustomed to take up his dwelling in the human spirit as the flesh yields ground.

## 5

Hence all of us who bear the new and royal name and are called Christians have received this fast as a paternal inheritance and cherish it. It begins on the second day [of the week], since it was ordained on account of matter, that it might consume matter and present the mind pure to the Pure One; and it ends on the Day of Preparation, the day that completes the world's creation. Thus we learn that the purpose of fasting is the completion of divine works and the perfecting of our own little world, so that the human being within us may be fully equipped for every good work.

What day do I mean? The day after which comes the radiant raising of Lazarus; after that, the One who destroys our enemy and avenger, death—so called because death was imposed as the penalty for our sin—receives from the mouths of infants and nursing children the praise that was truly perfected. For praise from such mouths is a perfect acclamation: it cannot be slandered as something contrived in merely human fashion rather than by a higher power.

## 6

“What then?” you will say. “Shall we not count the days after that Friday as fast-days just as those before it?” Certainly we shall. But we do not count them as part of the days already traversed. They form something distinct and exceptional: an ascent corresponding to the sufferings which the Impassible One endured for us. Since those sufferings bestowed on us an exceptional benefaction, they too should be honored by us with a distinction of their own.

For this reason the fast up to that Friday is bounded by the fast of the Lord in the wilderness. After it we glorify the miracle wrought upon Lazarus, not celebrating it sooner, lest we introduce festivity while the appointed fast is still in progress, but awaiting the completion of the fast and only then celebrating the mystery of the raising of the Master's friend. For after fasting, and out of fasting, comes also the raising of the dead things within us.

The following days were appointed as days of fasting for those awaiting the resurrection of the Lord. To introduce relaxation by loosening the form of the fast precisely while awaiting that resurrection would be exceedingly unreasonable and ungrateful, showing no perception of the majesty of what God the Word suffered in the flesh for us. It would be, in the Wise Man's phrase, introducing music into mourning at the wrong time. Indeed, some of those whose spirit burns with greater fervor are found consecrating all the days close to the Passion by complete abstinence from food.

## 7

If something should also be said concerning the number of the fast-days, we shall find that here too nothing is without reason: neither the pattern given beforehand in Moses, nor the course completed by Elijah, nor the perfection given by Christ the Word, nor the tradition handed on to us after Christ and from Christ.

The more perfect account of the number we shall learn when we ourselves bear nothing imperfect. Then, as perfected friends, we shall eat and drink together at the table of the all-perfect Father, strengthened in heart by the breads of Wisdom, by her sacrifices and the wine she has mingled in her mixing bowl, carried outside ourselves in divine ecstasy—seized rather than seizing.

For the present, however, I contemplate the matter as one may see the breadth of heaven reflected in the tiny pupil of an eye. We have been appointed to fast forty days so that we may neither know the commandments apart from the practice of the virtues nor practice the virtues outside the commandments. Rather, multiplying and increasing the one through the other, we ourselves may become stars of God, shining in word and life and judged worthy to be numbered by him.

## 8

For we know ten principal commandments and four principal virtues. Thus to know the words of the commandments while doing nothing in accordance with them makes a man like that wicked and lazy servant who possessed the talent but put it to no use. Conversely, to act, but outside the law of the commandments, is profitless and without reward, for “no one is crowned unless he competes according to the rules.” He competes lawfully who pursues righteousness righteously.

We must therefore accept neither naked knowledge of doctrine and the commandments—for faith without works is dead—nor irrational and untaught activity, for works too are dead without faith. But when each is joined and mingled with the other, as soul with body, they display the human being who is complete according to God.

The Jew too fasts, but he has heard, “Your fast, your idleness, and your feasts my soul hates.” Why? “Your silver is counterfeit.” For where is the tested word among those who murdered the Word? This too is laid to their charge: “Your hands are full of blood”—hands that not only failed to work what is good but went on to kill the Lord. Those from the heresies also fast, but without crown and without prize, because their contest is not lawful. An Orthodox man, on the other hand, may get drunk—and he will receive many blows, because he knew his Master's will and did not do it.

## 9

Therefore, brothers, together with knowledge of the commandments we must embrace the virtues. This is what the number of the fast-days symbolically means for us: multiplying the four by the ten, and the ten by the four.

Since enough has now been said about the fast—and since the builders of the Church did not arrange even this without wisdom—come, let us speak as far as we can about the veneration of the Cross. That the instrument of our salvation, consecrated by the divine body and blood, ought to be venerated is not a matter requiring investigation or an argument to establish faith. But someone with a more eager ear may desire, as a kind of seasoning, an explanation of why it has been established that the Cross should be set forth publicly for veneration precisely at the midpoint of the fast: neither before this point nor afterwards, but in the middle.

## 10

Surely this too is the work of the Spirit. For the Spirit knows human nature: at the beginning it usually burns more warmly, but as it advances it does not maintain an even motion; it grows numb, so to speak, and becomes industrious at doing nothing. Therefore, lest we begin the fast vigorously and then slacken as we go forward, they set the Cross in the midst: receiving the labors already completed and strengthening us for those still to come.

It is like what happens in athletic contests when those who encourage the competitors by shouted exhortations rouse them when they are exhausted—or seem to be—and often station themselves in the middle of the course. The Cross becomes for us both a remembrance of the sufferings of the great God and Savior and something like a trainer saying to the runner who has run well:

“If the Master was nailed to a cross, given gall to drink, pierced in the side, and endured every deed and every name of dishonor, do not collapse yourself. Let not your feet be shaken by the hand and activity of sin; let not your steps be poured away. Run with endurance the contest set before you. Run not uncertainly: the resurrection is manifest, and the Cross leads you by the hand toward it—the impassible and most precious prize of sufferings and dishonor, forever shining upon the Cross.”

## 11

From our present veneration we are also taught the mediation which the Cross mediated for us, reconciling us again to God the Father, against whom through disobedience we had made ourselves enemies.

For the enemy had wrestled us down with two hands, as it were: pleasure and pain. When we had fallen in a fall worthy of pity, the Dawn from on high visited us through the compassionate mercy of our God. At once, from the very beginning, the adversary was maimed. Pleasure did not stain Emmanuel's conception: no iron ascended to cut virginity, no human hand and no marital act approached Mary's divine altar. Nor did anguish constrict his birth, because pleasure had not preceded it.

Where pleasure goes first, pain inevitably follows; and where pain comes first, pleasure follows after. This is a law of nature, unbroken and indissoluble, ordained in philanthropy so that what delights us should not be unbridled and masterless, nor what bites us incurable and deadly: the former has the pain that follows as admonition and a restraint upon insolence; the latter has sweetness as a physician against despair.

## 12

Then, when he was manifested at the Jordan, he accepted from a servant the cleansing undertaken for us servants, and for our sake received the Spirit—he who had never been separated from the Spirit—in order to show the Spirit borne upon the water when we too are baptized, and to persuade us to run to such a bath as a true and perfect purification, a bath that receives the Spirit of God.

He is led by the Spirit into the wilderness, for the one who has been cleansed needs freedom from disturbance, lest he be muddied again by the slime of the mire and require a second cleansing. There he receives the tempter's assaults through pleasure, not by way of interior deliberations—for the mind of God is inaccessible to these—but by the tempter approaching him directly. By the fall of the one who wrestled against him he is crowned for our sake and ministered to by angels, so that you may learn the honor with which you will be honored when you defeat the one who is dishonored among the angels.

Having conquered pleasure and bestowed the victory upon his brothers, only then did he set out to teach. For he had first to prepare and cleanse human nature as though it were a vessel, so that it might become capable of containing the Word: casting down love of glory after gluttony, and love of wealth after love of glory—the three daughters of the leech that have sucked away our life. At the same time he taught us who are teachers not to undertake the word of instruction before defeating the tempter through practice.

## 13

One hand still remained to the enemy: pain. From then on he used it. He set up against Christ the Pharisees, Sadducees, scribes, and all those who rose against him. He slandered him with words, pelted him with stones, proposed malicious sophistries—what did he not contrive against him? At last he arranged for him to be numbered with lawless men and prepared the most dishonorable death for him, publicly shaming him so that not only would the one hanging on a tree be regarded as accursed, but every form of abuse and outrage customary among lawless men would be displayed against him.

I consider this trial, the trial through pain, harder to defeat than the former one, insofar as falling into pain is more grievous than merely being deprived of pleasure. The tempter himself shows this, for he would not have applied this trial last had he not known it to be stronger.

Yet Christ passed through this trial too and conquered: not only enduring everything, but repaying his murderers with prayers. From that point our nature received an uncontestable victory, crowned through the deeds of the One who conquered the world. This is truly the most perfect trophy and the complete salvation procured for us by the Cross. Until the Cross, some hope still remained to the enemy, feeding those disposed toward him on pride.

## 14

From then on his hand remained powerless, and truly at that moment “the swords of the enemy failed utterly.” For through the death of the Son we were reconciled to the Father.

Adam died justly: death had been imposed upon him according to the covenant, “In the hour in which you eat from the tree, you shall surely die.” Death was the penalty for transgression and sin. But the Lord, having committed no sin—how could the one who did not come through pleasure and pain, from which and around which sin arises, and who plainly stood above them?—was put to death unjustly through the Cross. By that unjust death our just death was abolished. Rightly, therefore, do we inscribe our salvation upon the Cross.

One sinless man died for all, though he was not subject to death. For the sting of death is sin, like a sword in the hand of a soldier, readily piercing those who receive it without resistance. And this is the summit of philanthropy: the One who owed nothing discharged our debt.

The Lord himself indicated this in his final words: “The ruler of this world is coming, and he has nothing in me; but as the Father commanded me, so I do.” For God so loved the world that he gave his Son to death as its ransom. O depth of wisdom! O wealth of goodness! From love toward humanity comes the death of the Son; from that death comes again reconciliation and love toward humanity—so that the reconciliation might appear altogether just, as though God were paying a debt rather than merely granting a favor.

## 15

The Cross is the mediator and procurer of this reconciliation for us, and it now symbolizes that mediation by being set forth and venerated in the very middle of the Fast.

Or, if you wish, understand the Fast itself as Paradise, in whose midst the tree of life is now planted, equally available to all—unless someone has made the belly his god. Paul sets such a person down as an enemy of the Cross. Yet even this person the Cross calls and receives, to consume the swelling of his belly and persuade him not to surrender everything to its pleasures: let him look upon the gall and vinegar tasted by the One fastened there; let him cease stretching himself toward ease from every side as he looks upon the nails and the thrusts of the spear; let him put the finger of inquiry into these things and proclaim, instead of the belly, as God and Lord the Author and Perfecter of this bitter and painful way of life.

For the Cross was fixed in the first place on behalf of sinners who had offended God; and, joining together all spirits, it bound them to one another and to the Lord.

## 16

As the Cross has height and depth and an extension on either side across its breadth, so it gathered the things in heaven and the things on earth, from sunrise to sunset, into Christ who created all things. Thus there are no longer two creations—angels and children of men; things unshaken and things shaken; things in heaven and things on earth; those near and those far off—but one creation, all things made new in Christ: all made unshaken, heavenly, and brought near.

Such are the gifts of the Cross. They did not occur once only, nor appear briefly, but are always occurring and always appearing, proclaiming the majesty of the One who worked salvation upon it. They will cease only when the power of God becomes weak. As long as that power remains, strengthening the weak, know that the Cross possesses this same great power for those being saved. The Apostle himself bears witness that it is the power of God: “The word of the Cross is foolishness to those who are perishing, but to us who are being saved it is the power of God.”

## 17

The Cross is also called a weapon of God's good pleasure, given to the soldiers of Christ whom God wills to prosper, and a crown against their enemies. At once it arms and crowns; this too we learn from David. It is also a mark set upon those who fear God and have nailed their flesh [to it]. For there is no other mark by which they are known to belong to Christ than their crucifying the flesh with its passions and desires.

Paul bears witness, boasting in the Cross as though in a royal insignia, and thereby becoming recognizable no longer as Paul but as Christ: “I live, yet no longer I, but Christ lives in me.” And this, it seems, is what the Lord meant when he said that the one who chooses to follow him must deny himself and take up the Cross.

The Cross is also the wisdom of God, even if it is denounced as foolishness by the Greeks, for “the foolishness of God is wiser than men.” It is the footstool of his feet, as the resting-place of his incarnate sojourn; the place where the feet of the Lord came to a standstill, and with them the movements made for our salvation. For what further movement was needed once he had reached the goal and salvation—the purpose for which he came—had been accomplished?

## 18

Let no one, therefore, remain apart from the sight of it, from prostration before it, or from its veneration: sanctifying the eyes by looking, the other members by bending down, and the lips by touching it. Let the tongue be sanctified too, whether singing or teaching. Let the hearing be sanctified by receiving the sound of the things of the Cross and carrying them across into the mind.

Are you a king? Approach the scepter of Christ. Learn that you are a slave purchased at a price. Recognize the Master; look upon those under your hand as fellow-slaves, for we were bought with one blood. Learn, at the season of the Cross, what the outward forms honored in your kingship really are when they are acted out in mockery: the jewel-studded crown made rough with thorns and anxieties; your mighty scepter exposed as an empty reed; the purple over which you swell with pride shown to be another's possession and a thing born of blood—for soldiers put it around you. See the genuflections you receive and the acclamations, most of them feigned, coming from men who would gladly strike your head, and more gladly still cut it off.

Learn these things while venerating the Cross. For even mockery in Christ must become serious instruction, and from every direction we must be taught by the Word.

## 19

Have you been appointed a soldier? Take up the weapon of God's good pleasure, so that you may flee from the face of the bow—whether the intelligible bow, which shoots at the intelligible part of us and wounds through the appearance of sensible things those who are not defended against it, or the visible bow, which destroys the visible body.

Do you abound in the goods of the earth? Receive the Cross's exhortation. It shows your Teacher fastened upon it naked. You profess to be his follower: then tread also in his footprints. Take up your own cross and renounce everything.

Are you afflicted by poverty? Consolation is near. This very burden of poverty is for you an involuntary cross. You too have been compelled to carry it. Make the good voluntary, therefore, so that it may be more richly rewarded. You will do so if poverty does not drive you to tolerate any wickedness and if you bear your straitened condition with thanksgiving.

How much would you have labored and spent if a running contest had been set before you, merely to make swollen and heavy limbs appear leaner and lighter? Now you are already light without effort, freed from the dropsy of possessions. Do not therefore grow despondent because you are healthy in bodily leanness, nor pronounce blessed the empty swelling of the rich man according to the flesh, lest you reveal yourself poor in understanding as well.

## 20

The Cross will also educate those who shine in worldly glory, persuading them to look toward the Lord of glory whom the rulers of this age crucified. In their passion for lying glory they could not endure the true glory of his works. Let the glorious fear sharing their portion; let them take care not to kill Christ themselves for the sake of worldly reputation. Instead, let them seek the glory that comes from the only God, be clothed and crowned with it, and be formed according to the image of the Only-begotten's glory: becoming and appearing sons of God through their works, and shining to the glory of the heavenly Father.

Do you too ache because you are without honor and cannot endure seeing your neighbor prosper beyond you? What then? Do you not see the Crucified One—his dishonors, the spittings, the blows, the reputation of being a deceiver? Do you not recognize something lordly and divine in the One who was dishonored and spat upon in the world? And then see how he rose as king, clothed himself in majesty, was taken up in glory, sat at the right hand of the throne of Majesty, and will come in glory, when every knee shall bow to him, in heaven and on earth and under the earth, and every tongue confess that Jesus Christ is Lord, to the glory of God the Father.

## 21

Do you live in cheerfulness and ease? Look at the Cross and consider human weakness, unable to travel the road of ease without slipping and falling. Our luxuriance in Paradise gave birth to the sin for which the Immortal One was put to death. Reflect on these things; restrain yourself; shudder at the mystery enacted for you. Do not, by dissolving yourself in pleasure, make the Master's death useless for yourself, lest the Jesus being corrupted within you lament and say, “What profit is there in my blood, in my going down into corruption?”

Are you despondent? Consider the blessings of the Jerusalem above, of which through the Cross you have been judged worthy. Remember God and rejoice. In only a little while you will come to him and appear before his face; you will pass into the place of the wondrous tabernacle, amid a sound of exultation and thanksgiving, the noise of those keeping festival. Shake off worldly grief as filthy dust or the soot of smoke.

Rebuke the passionate part of your soul: “Why are you sorrowful, O my soul? And why do you trouble me?” But do not merely rebuke it; teach it also to hope in God, for hope is more divine and is grief's antagonist. And if you must be sorrowful about something, grieve that you once despaired over vanities, and show compassion toward brothers who are being crushed.

## 22

Become Jeremiah, or Paul, weeping over those wounded by sin, the enemies of the Cross. Groan over those perishing in Jerusalem and in the Church that is ours upon the earth, so that the sign you venerate may be set upon your forehead.

The philanthropy of the Cross was answered with ingratitude by those whom it benefited. Who ever bestowed benefits so great and received evils so great in return as Christ? Yet even so he asked forgiveness for the men who planned and accomplished those dreadful things against him: “Father, forgive them this sin,” he said, adding ignorance as the consideration most likely to obtain pardon. In this very act he stands—or rather offers himself—as priest on behalf of the people's sins committed in ignorance.

## 23

What then of us who stand at the altar? Do we learn only small things from the Cross? Or rather, things greater and more perfect in proportion as we are an image of Christ and repeatedly offer him beforehand for the people, renewing that perfect sacrifice accomplished once for all, using the Cross in everything, marking this people with it and sanctifying them?

Let us fear, then, lest while we profess to seal others with this seal against which no plot can prevail, we ourselves be found easy prey because we are unsealed, carrying no sign of a crucified life.

For monks, the Cross is their very counterpart. Their whole life is hardship and mortification, mourning that is called blessed, dishonor, and a falling down that raises them up, together with all the other things of this kind that are manifested in the Cross. Therefore, monk, what you embrace is not another's possession but your own treasure. Do not suffer others to see it and snatch it before you.

And you who belong to the people and to simplicity, do not stand far from the Cross. It was fixed for you as much as for anyone, and it is set before you. Fill yourself with it. Be overcurious about nothing else. Resolve to know nothing except Jesus Christ, and him crucified. When you lie down and rise, sit and stand, undertake some work or rest, begin a journey or finish it, taste food or cease from it—fortify yourself with this sign.

If you do so, you will not fear the terror by night, the thing that walks in darkness, the mishap and the noonday demon. For against all these the sign of the Cross is a means of deliverance; no medicine is so repellent to demons as this. Once crushed by it, they fear it. However shameless they may be, they cannot look directly upon its power. Flight is therefore their only safety.

## 24

The Cross of Christ even calls tax-collectors. Who knows whether they may entertain the Lord, spending their whole wealth upon the banquet, and themselves become a Gospel of Christ, displaying in their own persons the miracles and proclaiming the power and activity of the Cross?

Approach it with a more generous love. And if you are a robber, even a robber has some claim upon the Cross: it is accustomed to benefitting robbers as well. Confess the Crucified One to be king of all and king of yourself; act upon the confession; and you will hear that philanthropic voice: “Today you will be with me in Paradise.”

O goodness! O grace! The stains and blood-guilt of how many years were washed away by a single utterance of repentance and confession! Who will despair of himself when he sees a robber being saved and, together with the One who summons him, forcing his way into Paradise?

## 25

Let us all venerate; let us fall down—not merely bending the body but first bending the knee of the heart, marking our servitude by doing the Master's commandments and not merely by bodily prostration. The Jews too fashioned this outward appearance, yet they heard Isaiah proclaim against them: “This people honors me with their lips, but their heart is far from me.” And the Savior grants entrance into the kingdom not to the man who merely calls him “Lord,” but to the one who does his works.

Let us learn something more mystical from the bending of the knees. Every time we bend down and rise again, let us contemplate the Lord's death and resurrection; through this let us ourselves die to sin and rise to righteousness, falling away from what is worse and rising toward what is better, according to the great prophecy of Symeon concerning Christ.

The kingdom of heaven has been opened again. Even now it is within us whenever through the Cross we reign over the passions; later it will be revealed openly, when the Judge comes with the Cross to bestow the kingdom upon those who have prepared it for themselves by the works laid down within them—or rather, who have prepared themselves here below for it.

## 26

The banquet has been prepared for all: both the Pascha now celebrated at this mystical table, to which the Cross leads us, present and proclaimed among us; and that Pascha which will later be celebrated at the heavenly altar, from which those who serve the tabernacle and this passing world have no right to eat. We know that latter Pascha as the more perfect and more mystical one.

A kingdom lies before us: how wretched is the man who is left outside it! A banquet has been prepared, made sumptuous with oxen and fattened calves: who will not run to enjoy it? Yet deceivers pull us away in the opposite direction, just as they did those invited in the Gospel.

But our ally is not far from us. Let us shake them off with the Cross. Let fasting sharpen our appetite. Let us wash our hands in the blood shed by the slaughter of our sinful and fleshly mind, and let us feast upon the Lord, satisfied when his glory appears to us, now and always and unto the ages of ages. Amen.

### Selected textual notes

1. **§3, “two creations.”** The distinction is between creation into being (*to einai*) and moral re-creation from “being badly” to “being well” (*to kakōs einai* → *to eu einai*). The latter is central to the homily's ascetical logic.
2. **§5, “second day / Day of Preparation.”** The Greek speaks liturgically of Monday and Friday. The calendrical explanation is rhetorically compressed and depends on Byzantine reckoning of the creation week.
3. **§7–9, forty.** Theophylact derives forty symbolically from ten commandments multiplied by four principal virtues. The four are not enumerated in the surviving text at this point; he assumes the standard quartet of cardinal virtues.
4. **§11–14, pleasure and pain.** The paired terms are *hēdonē* and *lypē*. Theophylact treats them as the two “hands” by which the enemy wrestles down humanity, then reads Christ's conception, birth, temptation, and Passion as successive defeats of those powers.
5. **§15.** One word after “consume the swelling of his belly” is badly damaged in the PG OCR. The translation preserves the secure syntax without manufacturing a specific lost noun.
6. **§18.** The imperial paragraph deliberately interprets the mock regalia of the Passion as a critique of actual imperial regalia. Theophylact's language about courtiers who would gladly strike or decapitate the emperor is intentionally severe.
7. **§24.** The opening image probably alludes to Zacchaeus and/or Matthew: the “tax-collector” may convert wealth into hospitality and become, by transformed conduct, a living proclamation of the Gospel.

# II. On the Entry of the Most Holy Theotokos into the Temple

## 1

Even the commemoration of an ordinary righteous person, when accompanied by praise, has often exposed the one giving the praise by showing how far his words fall below the subject; speech does not always, nor in every case, equal reality. How then, when we undertake to commemorate with praise the Lady of every righteous person, the Mother of God the King of righteousness, could I discover a discourse that touched even some small part of her worth?

For I think that even if there are words proper to angels—words that are truly messengers of mysteries—not even these could reach the graces of the Mother of God. Her holiness has surpassed all creation; no speech belonging to a creature could hymn her worthily.

Shall we therefore be altogether silent? Shall we irrationally pass over the Mother of the Word, the one who procured for us the life according to reason? Or shall we say what lies within our power and bless her, as she herself foretold, venerating as far as we can the great things done for her? Better still: by these very failures of speech let us glorify the Ineffable One, proclaiming his greatness precisely through those things we cannot comprehend. In doing so we too shall receive some sanctification: those who speak, in their tongue; those who hear, in their hearing.

## 2

Human nature was in a miserable condition. Once torn away from God, it was carried headlong like a stone from a mountain peak, shattered and scattered among many opinions and notions, and through them wretchedly corrupted. Since the deception had taken its beginning from a woman, it spread through the whole race, sparing not even what was thought the stronger part of our nature.

What then? Our great Creator reveals himself also as Provider. By providence he confirms his work of creation, and the wisdom with which he governs creatures becomes the herald of the power by which he created them. For the maker must care for what he has made, if indeed he wishes it to endure rather than to receive dissolution at the very moment it is constituted. Otherwise creation itself would be irrational and artless—the work of a child playing in the sand, who is himself more played with than playing as he builds.

## 3

The Creator did not therefore leave us without providential care. He instructed and brought us together by conversations and manifestations; he made us wise through dreams; he benefited us in every manner; he gave the Law as an aid. Then he used harsher medicines and pressed us with heavier things, so that everything fleshly, superfluous, and incapable of housing the Spirit might be melted away. What did he not do? What means did he not devise?

When he saw that all these remedies proved weaker than our negligence, and that we had been struck from head to foot by bitter robbers, while neither the Law nor the Prophets could give us oil or bandages—passing us by, powerless because of the sin reigning in our flesh—his compassion compelled him himself, in the likeness of sinful flesh, to come among us. Condemning sin in the flesh and dying, one for all, he would bestow the victory upon our whole nature.

Death had reigned, escorted and strengthened by sin, and possessed great dominion over us, since we were conceived in transgressions and born in sins; and by a necessity of nature those who are born must also perish. Therefore even birth itself had to be sanctified, and the torrent of death flowing through the valley of sinful generation had to be stopped by the great bulwark of holiness.

## 4

Since this was so, the entrances into the present life had first to receive grace, so that ends corresponding to such beginnings might follow.

And so the woman who would minister to the divine birth was chosen: graced with every virtue and surpassing every creature. She was of royal stock, from Judah; sprung from parents illustrious in lineage, but more illustrious still in piety; and she appeared to them as a fruit given by God.

For such are God's judgments. They walked blamelessly in all his ways, yet they were in danger of bearing the reproach of childlessness—whether God was testing their faith, as with our forefather Abraham (for he is accustomed to lay heavier burdens on the stronger, so that they may receive more brilliant crowns and become consolation to those who come after), or whether he was indicating through this some deeper and more symbolic mystery.

At any rate their hearts were gnawed by it; the remembrance of childlessness tore them apart. Most of all the sword of barrenness cut the blessed Anna—for what is woman? A tender thing, readily yielding to sorrow, and especially stung by longing.

What then did they do? They did not flee to physicians. They did not bind themselves with amulets. They did not seek potions supposed to assist conception. They did not summon magicians. Such things belong to people truly diseased—people whose souls have been swallowed by unbelief and bound in the unspeakable knots of the Evil One, people who are in truth unworthy to leave seed behind them in this life.

Joachim and Anna did nothing of the sort. They looked to the Maker of creation, the One who from the beginning gave increase and multiplication and who made Sarah's dead womb living. They surrendered themselves to fasting; through prayers they drew down the divine goodness; they recalled the ancient wonders enjoyed by the wives of the patriarchs, by the Shunammite, and by Hannah the mother of Samuel. They sowed in tears and reaped in exultation. Those formerly despised because of their barrenness were changed, and a little daughter was born to them who surpassed them in holiness and grace by as much as God's dwelling and repose in her surpassed his dwelling in them. For in them God simply dwelt, according to the saying, “I will dwell in them and walk among them”; in her his indwelling would assume an altogether incomparable form.

## 5

Did they then fail to recognize the grace? Did they imitate ignoble parents? Hoping to possess in the child a guide for old age, an heir of their life, and a successor to their family, did they keep her at home and bring her up in luxury?

They could not truly have been her parents, nor would they have been judged worthy to receive her in the first place, had they been capable of thinking in this way. Rather, scraping away through the Spirit every attachment of the flesh and rising above all things, they gave back to God as a gift the good thing God had given them. By returning their blessing to him, they showed that it was from him that they possessed it.

They carried her to the Temple, a living and moving votive offering to the Lord. Beauty was added to the house of the Lord; the place was allotted to her who would become the dwelling of Glory—the place which David confessed that he loved but was not judged worthy to see with his eyes. The Child forgot her father's house and was brought to the King who desired her beauty.

Nor was she brought without boldness, or without honor and glory. She left the house in a splendid procession, everyone applauding her departure with acclamations. All her relatives and neighbors and all bound to the family by friendship accompanied her parents. Fathers rejoiced with the father, mothers with the mother; maidens and young girls went before the Child of God carrying lamps, like a circle of stars shining together with the moon. All Jerusalem rang with the sight of this new procession: a three-year-old little girl surrounded by such glory and honored with such a blaze of light.

When the Temple received them, the whole priestly order came out to meet them, welcoming her with hymns. Even the high priest himself was moved by the wonder—and all the more because he was more filled with God than the others.

## 6

Her parents brought the Child to him and entrusted her to him. They told him of their barrenness and of the promise made concerning her; but they confessed also that the upbringing of the Child lay beyond their own powers. Since she was something more divine, she required an education corresponding to her nature. Such a bright and priceless pearl must not be fitted to a poor rag; it ought rather to be woven into the royal robe, for greater glory and adornment.

Then the high priest, carried yet further outside himself and seized by God, recognized the Child as truly a dwelling-place of divine graces, more worthy than he himself to appear continually before the face of God. He understood that what the Law says deeply and mysteriously concerning the ark—that it should be deposited in the Holy of Holies—pointed clearly toward this Maiden.

Without hesitation, without shrinking back, he dared something righteous beyond the letter of the Law—or rather, rising above the lower law and passing through the darkness of the letter, he came to be with the Spirit. Carrying the offering into the Holy of Holies, he placed her there.

And the place received a Maiden which no other man was permitted even to see, which none of the priests might enter, nor even the high priest himself except once [each year]. For the one who had been sanctified in purity beyond all nature and justified from the womb ought not to be enslaved to the heaviness of a law “laid down not for the righteous” but imposed upon those who sin. Law entered because of transgressions and was set as a tutor over those who needed tutoring. But for her, who stood above even the angels, it was not law but grace that accomplished the more perfect things.

God himself signified through what followed that these events pleased him. He employed an angel as minister for the nourishment of the dedicated Child, and in a wondrous manner fed the one who would give him birth and feed him, so that scarcely anything in her might appear merely human but all things divine.

## 7

This is our festival; this is what we celebrate today: the bringing of the Maiden and her entry into the Holy of Holies.

O strange deed and strange report! A female child dwelling in God's inaccessible and unseen sanctuary! Even if she had merely trodden the outer court continually, that alone would have displayed her intimacy with the Master, since the words “You shall no longer tread my court” were spoken by God to those who had offended him. Had she merely seen the Holy Place, that would itself have been a great pledge of boldness before God. Had she entered once a year, that too would have far exceeded the lowly condition then assigned to women.

But now she passes beyond the court, crosses the second veil, enters the Holy Place, and is set apart to dwell continually with God. She becomes for human nature a pledge in advance of the grace that would follow. In herself she displays beforehand what is ours and opens the road for the whole race toward the ascent and entrance into the heavenly and true Holy of Holies.

Already in her the Law is shown to be passing away. Unable to justify or cleanse from sins, it kept almost everyone outside the holy places. But Christ was about to justify all by grace, break down the dividing wall of the barrier, open to everyone the entrances that formerly could not be trodden, sanctify all and cleanse them by water and Spirit, and admit them into the Holy of Holies. For this reason he receives the Virgin beforehand. The things now accomplished in her he gives as trustworthy pledges of the reconciliation of the whole race with himself that will follow.

## 8

She is dedicated as a child because of innocence and simplicity: “Of such is the kingdom of heaven,” and “The Lord upholds the innocent,” and his face beholds uprightness.

She is female because of Eve, through whom and from whom transgression came, so that where sin abounded grace might abound beyond measure: woman drove our nature from Paradise; a woman now goes before our nature into the Holy of Holies.

She is three years old, the number mystically indicating the Trinity—or rather, showing the holiness that had grown within her beneath the sign of the Trinity.

What does the procession mean? The bold and public character of grace, which has gone out into all the earth and is no longer enclosed within one place according to the narrowness of the Law.

What of the young maidens? They are souls belonging to the new lump according to God, accompanying the Virgin by imitation.

And I see something in their lamps as well: the light of life shining to the glory of God, and the illumination of knowledge kindled and held in hands active in good works. For the man whose hands hang slack, whose strength has abandoned him—as divine David says—lacks even the light of his eyes. The Holy Spirit flees deceit and will not dwell in a body burdened by sin.

## 9

But you—sit beside the shadow if you wish. Be scandalized at what has been said because of your own blindness. Think nothing beyond the letter, stiff-necked man, uncircumcised in heart and ears, forever resisting the Spirit.

Is it credible that a high priest should so openly transgress the ordinary rule and undertake something so hazardous unless he had been persuaded of something more divine concerning the Child? How could he have brought the Maiden in so daringly unless he possessed counselors wiser than you—revelation and truth themselves—with which his breast was adorned and from which he received the signal of what must be done at every moment?

And which of the priests would have tolerated this? How would such an innovation not have stirred up the people—especially a people prone to faction and ever lying in wait for innovations? Yet God's will was stronger. What the holy God ratified no human being had power to scatter. By the ineffable reins of Providence the jaws of all were guided and restrained, and they praised what was happening as something beyond human calculation.

But perhaps you do not assent to these things as true, nor believe God in this matter. Then you possess something in common with your ancestors, a venerable paternal inheritance: unbelief in God. Of them the One they refused to believe said, “For forty years I was grieved with that generation, and I said: They always go astray in their heart.”

Take care, however, that you do not open a still wider door to the Greeks. In charging headlong against our traditions because you disbelieve the wonders of God under the Law, and while imagining that you are defending the Law, you may discover that your judgments actually speak against it.

## 10

Let the Jews, then, carry the veil over their eyes and see nothing luminous or worthy of God. They closed their eyes and therefore did not know where they were walking in darkness.

But let us, with unveiled face beholding as in a mirror the glory of the Lord, marked and formed by his light, not merely contemplate and honor the things of the feast. Let us ourselves become a feast—or the material of a feast.

How shall this happen? Yesterday your soul was barren and fruitless, bringing forth no child worthy of the light of life. Today receive into the womb the Master's longing: through good fasting, tears, confessions, and the seeds that are truly good, bring forth for yourself a spirit of salvation. Nourish the child and make it grow through faith in the venerable Trinity. Introduce neither a plurality of gods—for that is vulgar—nor a deficient conception of monarchy: the first belongs to Greek shame, inventing successions within deity; the second to Jewish smallness, unable through the narrowness of its reasoning to make room for the Three.

Then give the Lord his votive offering, since every good gift comes from him. Do not attribute the good offspring to your own labors, but to his power and grace.

Let the light of virginity—or, for those in another state, chastity—shine upon you unquenchably. Then you will be worthy to enter, or to bring others, into the Holy Place. For without sanctification and purity no one will see the Lord: believe Paul speaking together with the Lord. Thus you will be fed on the divine and mystical bread, brought and given to you through an angel—if, as we say and believe, the priest is an angel of the Lord.

## 11

Such are the profits of a Christian festival; this is how Christians are made radiant by feasts.

Are you a virgin? See the glory of virginity: where it brings and raises a person, with what it feeds her and through whom. Do not let your foot be given over to tossing waves. Do not allow your lamp to grow drowsy. Do not let death climb in through your windows. Do not profane the holy thing of incorruption, for virginity does not admit restoration. Who has ever restored virginity to himself after its loss, even if he melts away his flesh and guards himself thereafter so that thieving thoughts cannot mount upon him?

Have the bonds of marriage instead been placed around you? Do not accuse marriage of separating you from God, and do not offer empty excuses. Joachim and Anna were under marriage and yet were not outside God. And here is the strangest thing: because they had obtained one who would continue their family and then let her go, their intention became itself without need of succession. Giving their daughter to God, they received in return the privilege of becoming and being called the parents of the Mother of God.

Look toward them. Give to marriage what properly belongs to marriage, as to Caesar, and give to God the things that are God's. Love your wife as your own flesh—for no one ever hated his own flesh. Let your eyes extend no farther than your own household. Drink water from your own cisterns; the waters of another are narrow indeed and bring affliction upon the flesh even here through the laws.

Bring up your children in the discipline and admonition of the Lord, fearing the condemnation of Eli the high priest. Then you have been sanctified through marriage and have sanctified marriage; you have become skillful with both hands, handling the things of the world according to God and making Scripture true in your own case: “A wife is joined to a man from the Lord”—a joining that is truly good and crowned by God, who fits all things together according to reason.

## 12

Thus let us keep festival, brothers; thus let us ourselves be made into a festival, so that we too may be judged worthy of entrance into that more perfect Holy of Holies: virgin souls, cleansed of every evil—for the word recognizes this as true virginity—holding our lamps bright with the oil of philanthropy, into the place where Christ has entered for us as forerunner.

May we have as helper both in intention and in deed the very one who has judged us worthy of so many graces, who now sanctifies us through her festival and will judge us worthy of the beauty of the all-holy Trinity, deeper within than all things: Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, the one God, to whom belong all glory, honor, and veneration, now and always and unto the ages of ages. Amen.

### Selected textual and interpretive notes

1. **§1, “life according to reason.”** Theophylact plays on *Logos* / *logos*: Mary is Mother of the divine Word and, through him, the one who procures for humanity life ordered according to reason.
2. **§3, the wounded traveler.** The image of humanity stripped and beaten while Law and Prophets pass by is an implicit Christian reading of the parable of the Good Samaritan.
3. **§4–6.** The narrative of Joachim, Anna, Mary's dedication at age three, angelic feeding, and residence in the Holy of Holies is not narrated in the canonical New Testament. Theophylact receives the developed Byzantine tradition and interprets it typologically.
4. **§6, “something righteous beyond the letter of the Law.”** The Greek is deliberately paradoxical: the high priest *tolmā ti para ton nomon dikaion*—“dares something righteous contrary to/beyond the law.” The following sentence resolves the paradox by distinguishing the lower letter from the Spirit.
5. **§7.** The argument is ecclesiological as much as Marian: Mary's entry anticipates the opening of the true Holy of Holies to the entire human race through Christ.
6. **§9.** The polemical addressee is a hypothetical literalist/Judaizer rather than a named contemporary opponent. Theophylact deliberately uses biblical invective (“stiff-necked,” “uncircumcised in heart and ears”).
7. **§11, “virginity does not admit restoration.”** This is an ascetical claim about the bodily state, not a denial of repentance or forgiveness; the argument immediately turns to the sanctity possible within marriage.

# III. A Discourse to One of His Disciples concerning the Charges against the Latins

## 1

Your request has come before me, my most reverent son in the Lord. I judged it good and worthy of an archpastor's attention, yet at the same time I saw it being thrust away by the present circumstances, as though by a harsh and graceless doorkeeper. For you asked us to answer, briefly and as far as possible, the errors of the Latins in ecclesiastical matters—errors which, as you said, are many and possess no little power to divide the Churches. I know that almost everyone else thinks this way about them. I myself do not.

I do not know their errors to be either so numerous or of such a kind as to divide Churches—except for the one point that touches the very substance of our faith. Yet I hesitate to set myself against so many people and to trouble souls that are easily muddied, because love has grown cold, something the present age, as we have heard, has produced first of all in me by multiplying lawlessness. We no longer receive what a brother proposes in a brotherly spirit. We approach it as adversaries, and each man hurries to make a reputation for himself by elbowing aside whoever got there first. We imagine that the multitude will rank us foremost among those wise in divine things if we can smear some additional heresy upon our neighbors; and we think we shall prove ourselves keen-sighted if we manage to introduce some black-beaming Lucifer.

For these reasons it has seemed to me that this is a time for silence, as the ancient prophecy says. Yet from another side I have considered the fair-mindedness of your love, which ought not to suffer loss because of the maliciousness of others. So I am content to let the Egyptians sit idle beneath darkness that can be felt and encounter blood in their water, while I gladly see the whole Israelite people enjoying light for their work and water for their life. In short, I rejoice to see the same divine wonders become punishment for the one and salvation for the other.

## 2

The Latins, then, as I said, appear to be in error about many things. They offer unleavened bread; they fast on Saturday; they reckon the fast before the Passion differently from us; they dissolve the marriages of the ordained, while allowing marriages among the laity indiscriminately and with almost no barrier. And, if you promise not to laugh, they shave their beards—laymen and sacred orders alike. The hands of their clergy glitter with golden rings, and they dress themselves in priestly robes woven from silken thread and variegated with colors. Their ascetics eat meat; and when they have to bow before the Lord, they flatten themselves against the floor. In this last matter, indeed, all of them offend alike. You may even see Sicilians feasting upon things strangled—and then, if you follow this method of reasoning, you must abhor the Roman Church as though she herself taught such practices.

Perhaps one of our exceedingly faithful and hotter spirits, ablaze with zeal for orthodoxy, will leap up at this point and condemn us for ignorance, for lack of discrimination in divine things, or for coldness and betrayal of our own traditions. He might enumerate still more accusations than those I have mentioned. But of the things already listed I judge some to require no correction whatever, and others only a moderate correction: one which, if it accomplishes something, gives the Church a small benefit, but which, if it accomplishes nothing, does not turn the unattainable into a source of injury.

There is, however, one matter that seems to me above all to make communion with the Latins impossible for those who understand it, and whose failure to be corrected causes great damage to the inheritance which the Son received among the nations. This I shall show you and examine as far as I can. And if the discourse does not become too long, we shall also look, in due measure, at some of the other matters.

## 3

Their greatest error, then—the one that, to borrow Solomon's language, fastens a man to the snare of Hades—is the innovation they have made in the Symbol of Faith by proclaiming that the Spirit proceeds from the Father **and from the Son**.

The Symbol belonging to the faithful ought itself to be a true symbol, free of every adulteration. Not even the axe-bearing executioners in Ezekiel would have spared those who were marked unless they saw upon them a genuine and uncounterfeited sign.

How, then, shall we meet the innovation? Simply, plainly, and altogether as befits disciples of fishermen. Had we appointed some other teacher, we could use his words to persuade the disciples of that same teacher when they defended contrary doctrines. But since those who from the beginning were eyewitnesses and ministers of the Word handed this very Word down to us—saying of the Spirit of truth that he “proceeds from the Father”—the argument is simple. Either introduce another teacher who speaks more clearly than the Word and more wisely than Wisdom, from whom you received this novel doctrine and embraced it with such extraordinary affection; or, if you have no such teacher, receive our one Instructor, Christ, teaching about the Spirit who is of one kindred with him: from whom, and in what manner, the Spirit has his being. Receive Christ bearing witness concerning his own Witness that he proceeds from the Father.

For if, as you say, he also proceeds from the Son, what prevented Christ from saying, “who proceeds from the Father and from me”? As matters stand, you are found not following Christ, the evangelist of the poor, even though you say that you carry his cross.

If, however, some approved Father persuaded you of this, show us that Father, and that is enough for us. But you will not be able to show him, however hard you labor. Either the approved Father did not say it, or the man who said it is not approved. And even if we conceded that you had found such a passage, we should not draw what is rare into a universal rule. This principle too is one that you yourselves recognize. When ten thousand theologians speak of the Holy Spirit and none says anything of this kind, but rather that the Spirit was manifested **through the Son** and is **from God through the Son**, while one man, or perhaps two, is found introducing the contrary, to attach oneself not to the multitude but to those isolated voices is already heavy with the danger of error.

## 4

“But Scripture says,” someone replies, “the Spirit of the Son, and the Spirit of Christ.” I grant it. For he is also called the Spirit of truth and the Spirit of life, while the Son is truth and life; and I shall add the Spirit of wisdom and of power, since the Son is these things too. Yet none of these expressions means that the Spirit derives his existence from the Son. They mean that he is proper to him—kindred, not alien—that he rests upon him, and that from him he is sent, bestowed, and communicated to those who are worthy.

Indeed, the argument has done us a service by advancing to the very point that matters most. For it seems to me, you who set your minds on the things above, that you go wrong not so much through wickedness of intention as through ignorance of the correct distinction. You suppose that **to proceed** is equivalent to **to be bestowed** or **communicated**. Consequently, because the Spirit is found being sent, bestowed, and communicated from the Son, you imagine that no obstacle remains to saying that he also proceeds from the Son.

But that is not how things stand. Why? Because the expressions differ in every respect. “To proceed” indicates the manner in which the Spirit *is*. Just as the Son is from the Father by generation and not by some other manner, so the Spirit is from the same source—I mean the Father—not by generation, for there are not two Sons, but by procession. Procession, therefore, is the mode according to which the Spirit has his being from the Father; and this is recognized as his personal property.

Being sent, bestowed, and communicated, by contrast, do not indicate the manner in which the Spirit has his being. What they manifest is rather a certain enrichment, an outpouring of goodness: goodness whose being is from the Father, but which is poured out from the Son upon those who are worthy. In this sense the Spirit is also said to have been manifested through the Son. For the Spirit becomes manifest to those who have received the Word, not to those without the Word; and through the Son we have clearly learned the divinity of the Spirit, which before this was not openly known, since the Son placed him alongside the Father and himself in the baptism that makes divine.

So too the Lord is said after the resurrection to have breathed the Spirit into the disciples, not as though he were the source from which the Spirit's hypostasis is projected. The breathing was not, so to speak, the Spirit's coming into being. Besides, the Spirit was not then given in his total plenitude, but one gift—the forgiveness of sins. The full gift had been appointed for Pentecost. The Son possesses the Spirit essentially and gives him when he wills, to whom he wills, and as he wills: to some according to a single form of gift, to others according to several, and to others the “other Paraclete” himself in fullness.

Let us therefore stop forcing incompatible things into identity: communication and procession.

## 5

Perhaps you will tell me that you yourself know this distinction: that the Father is cause of the Spirit's being according to the mode of procession, just as he is cause of the Son's being according to generation; and that when you say the Spirit “proceeds” from the Son, you do not use the term in that sense but mean that he is poured forth and distributed. Perhaps your understanding is sound and you are driven into this terminology only by poverty of vocabulary and by the narrowness of the Latin tongue.

If that is the case, then here I shall show you the poverty blessed by the Spirit and the welcome due to a brother, and you in turn will abhor the uncleanness of a lofty heart. Thinking the same thing in one Spirit, we shall glorify with one accord Father, Son, and Holy Spirit: the Father as begetting and bringing forth, the Son as begotten, the Spirit as proceeding.

If in truth your language is poor in expressive wealth, and you lack garments for thought and speech, so that you cannot distinguish the outpouring or communication of the Spirit that comes from the Son—call it by whatever other name you please—from the procession according to which the Spirit has his being from the Father alone, then in other contexts I shall allow you to use the terms your language supplies: in ordinary conversation, and even, if you wish, in ecclesiastical discourse. But only with the proper qualification, so that your hearers are not left unaware that two different concepts are concealed beneath one word.

In the Symbol, however, proclaim the procession **from the Father alone**. For there we are making the confession of faith, and it must be pure, luminous, and simple—because the realities believed are themselves such—and must carry within it nothing confused, obscure, or double-edged. Such indeed is the exposition of faith we possess: the utterance of the Spirit who prevailed at the Second Council, breathed into the Fathers and producing their concord concerning himself, and preserved by all the Churches. Their dignity will not allow you to suppose that they think more meanly than you, for their rank is equal to yours; and their number gives them the stronger claim, for they are more numerous.

## 6

Add this consideration. If the Spirit's being bestowed from the Son is not something different from his proceeding from the Father, one of two consequences follows: either the Son too is cause of the Spirit, or the Father is only a giver of the Spirit in the same way as the Son.

If the Son is cause of the Spirit, then the one Spirit has two principles. And whatever requires several causes for its existence must, in relation to that which has its being from one, be either greater, equal, or less. Equal it cannot be, for then it too would have its being from one. What remains, then, is that it be greater or less. The Spirit will therefore be either greater than the Son—and behold the novelty of the impiety, for none even of those who dared attack the Son has yet ventured to say this—or less than the Son, and Macedonius comes back to life.

If, on the other hand, the Spirit is merely bestowed from the Father as he is from the Son, either he too will be without a principle—and what then becomes of the Father's distinctive dignity, and how has his personal property become common?—or we shall have to seek another cause for the Spirit. Then a tetrad of persons will be introduced among us and a dyad of divinities: one divinity of the Three, and another belonging to the imported fourth, who proceeds for us out of your marvelous innovation.

Again, if “to proceed” is identical with “to be bestowed,” I ask what other word will signify the manner in which the Spirit is from the Father. Again, if the Father is greater than the Son with respect to being cause, then by the same reasoning he will be greater than the Spirit; but if the Son too is cause of the Spirit, then in this respect the Son will also be greater than the Spirit. Where have you discovered the Son spoken of as greater than the Spirit?

Again, if the Lord's breathing upon the disciples signifies, as you say, the procession of the Spirit from himself—and therefore the whole Spirit, not one particular gift—then at that moment he gave them the Paraclete. What then becomes of, “If I do not go away, he will not come to you”? Again, if it was the Paraclete himself who was given through that breathing, rather than the gift of forgiveness of sins—for Scripture is accustomed to call the gifts themselves “spirits”—then the coming at Pentecost was either the coming of the same Spirit and therefore superfluous, or the coming of another spirit, and we shall have to ask what this other spirit was.

These, then, are our arguments for holding that the Spirit proceeds from the Father alone and not also from the Son.

## 7

Other things have been said on this subject by men wiser and older than we are. I have employed none of them in the present argument, taking Paul's principle to heart: not to build upon another man's foundation and boast in what lies ready to hand. Instead, as far as possible, I have tried to make this whole argument my own exercise—if indeed our weakness possesses anything good of its own, rather than everything belonging to grace.

I hear that some men of our own day have composed books of many pages on this doctrine; perhaps they have brought gold, precious stones, and purple into the tabernacle of God. But receive from me for the moment these skins from my own gross flesh, if for no other reason because they have at least acquired their red color from the Word who became flesh for my sake and poured out his blood, that he might initiate me into the mysteries—among them the mysteries concerning the Spirit—and make me royal and anointed, sealing and anointing me with blood and Spirit.

## 8

Since many people display a zeal about the offering of unleavened bread hotter, as the saying goes, than fire, and would sooner surrender their souls than loosen their opinion about it—and since some even indulge their own passion, which Paul regards as a condemnation and a snare of the devil—we shall later say what ought to be said to such men, checking in some the excess of zeal and showing others how abject is the pride they imagine to be anything but abject.

Toward the Latins, however, we shall not falsify the meaning of “the first day of unleavened bread” by claiming that it was the tenth day of the month, when the lamb was selected. We know that the Law calls the fifteenth day the first of the unleavened breads, the day upon which the night of eating the Passover dawned. Hence one of our own divine teachers, explaining Matthew's phrase “the first day of unleavened bread,” says that “the first” there means the day before the unleavened breads. He knew the Law assigns the name to the fifteenth day, and therefore marked the evangelist's expression as something unusual.

Nor shall we drag the Lord into our case by representing him either as not having celebrated the legal Passover, so that we may prove he did not partake of unleavened bread, or as having celebrated it before the time when leaven had to be removed, so that leavened bread might still have been available. Some of our overbold controversialists have already dared to say such things. I will attach myself to none of them. It is not lawful to hurry to establish the mysteries of truth by means of falsehood. If Christ is the Way and the Truth, I shall neither willingly wander from the Way nor lie against the Truth about something that is not so.

What, then, do I say? That the Lord ate the legal Passover at the proper time, when the hour had come, prepared for him by the disciples and the master of the house—according to custom, plainly, and not otherwise. Had there been an innovation, the Gospel would have marked it, just as it marks other actions in which he rose above the Law, whether he himself or his disciples: making clay on the Sabbath, commanding a man to carry his pallet, rubbing ears of grain, eating bread with unwashed hands.

He first ate the legal Passover and then handed on to the disciples the mystery of his own Passover, obviously using bread that happened to be at hand. At that moment the bread available was unleavened. But because that bread was unleavened under the necessity imposed by the Law, it does not follow that we who enjoy the freedom that is in Christ must necessarily crave unleavened bread and slap leavened bread away.

Or let us entangle ourselves with the devotees of the letter in another fashion; no harm can come from carrying their argument to its conclusion. The bread at that supper was probably—indeed, more than probably—cheap bread, perhaps barley bread. Neither the host nor the guests lived delicately; they were companions of an austere manner of life and accustomed to barley loaves, with which they had even fed thousands, so that you might see poverty working wonders. What, then, prevents us now from demanding precisely such bread and banishing the fine-wheat loaf from the altar as though even now we heard the Lord saying, “If you offer me fine flour, it is vain”?

The same could be said about the cup. The wine then may well have been thin or somewhat rough, in keeping with the rest of God's earthly lowliness; today a fragrant vintage is often offered. Shall we dismiss the costly wine and approve only the one that smells of poverty? Must we demand Palestinian wine from the very district from which the Lord's cup was filled—and, unless I am merely stringing absurdities together, demand a cup of the very same clay as well?

This really would be poverty of mind. Perhaps you will next insist on receiving the mystery only after eating, while reclining, in an upper room, and demand every other circumstance that the occasion then required but which piety and faith have now reshaped into another liturgical form.

## 9

Someone who hears the book of Acts repeatedly mention the “breaking of bread,” and finds the same language in Paul, may reasonably conclude that the bread then broken mystically was of the same sort as that laid out for ordinary eating. Ordinary food was certainly not always unleavened bread; unleavened bread was reserved for specified days and sacrifices.

If these considerations are not conclusive—for the argument can also be pressed in the opposite direction—they nevertheless place, to my judgment, the greater weight on our side. And victory can belong to the side with the slightest advantage, just as a runner is crowned for being ahead by a single stride.

Let the multitude of God's ministers throughout the East shame you into reflection, together with the dignity of the Church in Jerusalem, from which, as from a spring, flowed the stream of faith and apostolic tradition—including the practice of offering leavened loaves. Why speak only of the East, where you may count Indians and Ethiopians as well? Look also toward Egypt and the regions of Libya around Cyrene, and even toward the parched interior, and you will see them all of one mind and walking by one rule concerning the loaves of the mystical offering.

Look also at the bread of the Church of Corinth. You will hardly strike that Church from the roll as though she failed to preserve the traditions, for the very man who handed them on praised her for preserving them. To vote against him would exceed every kind of arrogance—not to grieve you by calling it impiety.

You also hear, I know, your own Sardinians bearing witness against the offering of unleavened bread, though you pretend to be deaf. I hear them clearly; and I hear the old proverb too, “the witness is from home,” casting the white pebble in my favor.

Why then, when you legislate against so many—and I would add, such weighty—witnesses, do you imagine you commit no fault at all? Why do you lift your eyebrows while acting contrary to the custom of the Churches of God? You ought to yield along with the rest; instead you try to drag the rest along with you, and you accuse us, when we could, if we wished, accuse you with better right. We refrain, for this reason at least, from causing you trouble and from further exciting ambition and contentiousness. But you do not know how to spare those who are sparing you.

## 10

For if we wished to examine your Saturday fasting with equal severity, we could show it to follow neither apostolic nor patristic teaching. The apostles condemn those who fast on the Lord's Day or on Saturday—with the one specified exception—assigning excommunication to laymen and deposition to clergy. Surely you will not dismiss these canons as spurious intrusions, since the Sixth Council itself honored them. When that council defined doctrine, representatives of your own Agatho were present; and when it legislated in canons, Basil of Gortyna appeared with such authority as to fill the place of the whole synod on your side.

The Fathers at Laodicea too—whose canons the Sixth Council likewise received with every honor—declared that during the Forty Days bread should not be offered except on Saturday and Sunday. They would never have legislated this if they had regarded Saturday as a fast-day. That we are not guessing at their intention is shown also by their ruling that the birthdays of the martyrs—that is, their commemorations—should not be celebrated during the Forty Days except on Saturdays and Sundays. Such days belong to festivity, not to the affliction signified by fasting; and what is more radiant than the commemoration of martyrs?

Would you like me to produce a royal decree as well? That Basil whose sovereignty in word and Spirit seized the whole earth, when he preached on fasting to the fair and great Church of Caesarea made fair and great through him, reckoned the fast as five days and thereby exposes your six-day reckoning as an interpolation. One might therefore aim Isaiah's words rather neatly at you: “Not such a fast have I chosen, says the Lord.”

## 11

What needed to be said to the Westerners by one who would be both a disciple and a teacher of truth, concerning unleavened bread and fasting on Saturday, has now been said according to our ability.

Someone might think their practice concerning marriages also deserved discussion. I have no leisure, however, to stir up Camarina—especially because they bring against us the marriage of ordained men, and not that alone but, as they say, innumerable other charges. Contentiousness is like this: blind regarding what exists, painfully sharp-sighted regarding what does not; it turns the former into faults and the latter into accusations.

When the time comes to defend ourselves against the charges brought against us, that will also be the time to speak about marriage and ask whether the error lies with them, with us, or whether both practices may be defensible in relation to the end each side sets before itself.

It remains instead to admonish many among our own people: those who possess zeal, but not according to knowledge, and—what is worse—those who out of self-love tear apart the body of Christ. I shall address the simpler sort first.

## 12

Not everything is to be demanded of everyone, brothers, just as not everything is to be conceded to everyone.

Where failure to remove something carries the damage into the vital parts, we must resist it with hands and feet, as the saying goes, and exact its removal like a tax that admits no exemption, knowing that payment of this tax contributes no small advantage to the whole affair. But where the absence of something causes no great injury, while forcing its acquisition would cause the greatest injury, a discerning man who understands the laws of practical judgment leaves the matter as it stands. He exchanges small things for great ones, not great things for trifles.

I see physicians making exactly this judgment in treating bodies. When brain, heart, or liver is diseased, they bring every resource to bear—in regimen and in drugs. Sometimes, when the rational methods of their art accomplish nothing, they even turn to supposed natural antipathies, mutter barbarous charms, and hang a jumble of miscellaneous substances upon the sick man; and in doing so it is almost as though they themselves hang their hopes upon these aids as much as they hang the objects upon the patient.

If they see some less important part of the body harmed by such treatment, they make little account of it, or none. Naturally they would prefer that part too to remain perfectly healthy; but where both goods cannot be secured they choose the lesser evil rather than the greater. Even when one of the less authoritative parts is itself the primary seat of disease, they preserve the same judgment: if, while treating it, they discover that some vital organ is thereby being damaged, they stop the treatment.

The physicians of the beautiful body of the Church must preserve the same principle. When the faith or the dogmas are sick, I demand that they bring every diligence to the cure, resisting the causes of disease in word and deed, contributing whatever lies within their own power, and—rather, before all this—asking from above for the true antidote and pronouncing the incantation that overpowers everything: the name of our Lord Jesus Christ, who took our infirmities and bore our diseases and healed every sickness; who is God's Word and Wisdom and Power, his right hand and arm, and every other name of divine knowledge and strength.

If this treatment fails because the disease is stubborn and malignant—the sort Paul calls gangrene—then one must take up the iron of exclusion from communion or anathema, or employ cautery or cutting, so that the evil word may not have pasture and seize those who until then were healthy. Thus that master physician dealt with the gangrenes of his own day: some he handed over to Satan that they might learn not to blaspheme; others, who preached contrary to his teaching, he placed under anathema, even if they were persons of reputation or possessed some higher dignity. That is precisely why he introduces even an angel into the statement.

## 13

So too with the Westerners. If they go wrong in something belonging to dogma and thereby shake the faith of the Fathers—as with the addition concerning the Holy Spirit in the Symbol—then the danger is of the greatest kind. Whoever allows such a thing to remain uncorrected becomes himself inexcusable, even if they speak from the throne that lofty men set forth as lofty; even if they put forward Peter's confession; even if they add the beatitude pronounced upon him; even if they rattle before us the keys of the kingdom. The more they seem by these claims to honor Peter, the more they dishonor themselves, since they undo what he established and pull away the foundations of the Church which he is believed to uphold.

Here, then, be a fighter for me, though gentle in other matters. Gird on the sword of the Spirit. Use the word of God that divides flesh from spirit, worldly conceit from divine mysteries, and judges human and fallible thoughts. As a Levite and minister of God, pierce those who make idols of the fabrications of their own reasonings, set their own head up for veneration, and demand that everyone fall prostrate before the dignity of their authority.

## 14

But once their doctrine has been set right and the innovation has yielded to what is older, suppose they continue to cherish their unleavened breads and fasts and leap away when we ask, in a spirit of gentleness, that they become of one mind with us even in these matters. Then become Paul to those under the Law, appearing as under the Law; join men who have taken a vow in purification, shave your head, pay the expenses for their sacrifices, circumcise Timothy—even though you are the same Paul who counted righteousness in the Law as refuse, who showed the Galatians that if they accepted circumcision Christ would profit them nothing. Paul did such things because he was contriving to gain those many thousands in the Church at Jerusalem and to still the storm raised against his preaching by the appearance of disagreement.

Helmsmen act in the same way: they ease the sheet when the sail is being submerged, empty it, and so save the crew together with the ship. I demand the same skill of your own steering. Do not keep everything taut—least of all where the wind of conceit and national arrogance blows excessively. Know how to slacken. Make the voyage, though slower, a voyage that preserves life rather than one whose speed is fatal. Preserve the voyage by yielding, rather than manufacture shipwreck by refusing ever to give way.

It would be best if the body of the faithful were not only complete in all its members but possessed beautiful hair and nails as well, like the body of a king's daughter, the bride of Christ—Christ beautiful in comeliness beyond the sons of men, himself whole and perfect, delighting to be joined to what resembles him. But it is still a great good if the body lacks none of its members, above all its eyes, by which we behold the sun of the Trinity and through that light everything else required for the constitution of life in the Spirit, even if its hair and nails do not match the beauty of the rest: if the hair is somewhat darker than one might desire, not quite like David, ruddy and beautiful-eyed, or like the bride's brother, white and fiery; if the nails are a little too long or too short, so that tiny splinters are grasped awkwardly or not at all.

You will not despise the rest of the body because of such things. On the contrary, engrossed in the beauty of its better parts and fastening your eyes upon them, you will scarcely notice the defects or turn your gaze elsewhere.

In this way you will burn more fervently in the Spirit and render to the Lord the service of a good and faithful servant, if you show yourself solicitous for the Master's household. You are solicitous for it when you cannot endure to see it suffer loss. It suffers loss when its fullness is diminished. Its fullness is diminished when we drive away our fellow servants. We drive them away when we harden ourselves against them. We harden ourselves when we refuse condescension.

Let us therefore condescend, lest we prove hard. By refusing hardness we shall receive others; receiving them, we shall fill the Lord's house; filling it, we shall make it richer; making it richer, we shall show our goodwill; by that goodwill we shall be attested good and faithful servants; and as such we shall enter into the joy of the Lord. Do you see to what height condescension has carried us?

## 15

We shall not, therefore, harden ourselves in return against the unbending temper of the nation over unleavened bread or fasting. That would be trying to glue potsherd to potsherd without putting between them something softer that might actually serve as adhesive.

Still less need we make much of the other accusations already enumerated, many of which the Latins themselves profess to observe. I except the eating of strangled animals: even the name of that practice is intolerable to sober Latins, just as the names of robbery or fornication are intolerable among us, even though beastly and brutish men among us may practice them.

Many people regard the remaining differences as unforgivable errors. In my judgment, no man well exercised in ecclesiastical history will agree. He has learned that **not every custom has power to divide Churches; only that which leads to a difference in dogma does so**.

The supposed errors condemned by our admirable judges are, after all, customs. Some arose from reverence—as, for example, the practice of bringing one's kisses down to the holy pavement. For surely we shall not accept that satanic slander which claims the Latins reject the veneration of images. Others arose from an economy meant to build up weakness, perhaps weakness of soul and certainly weakness of body—as when monks first began to partake of meat in illness, doing so with restraint and in a genuinely spiritual manner. If some men vulgarize the concession through indiscriminate use, that is another argument, directed against the abusers rather than against those who first arranged the matter reasonably.

Other customs were implanted in the Western Churches for other reasons. None of these has power to tear us apart—provided, of course, that those who judge them cast their vote in conformity with the laws of the Fathers.

## 16

Had my discourse not already become long and almost turned into a history, I could show you countless customs upon countless customs that the ancient Fathers tolerated for the sake of winning their brothers' souls. They knew not to please themselves, but each to please his neighbor for his good, unto edification.

But we now—in the swelling of our dropsical pride—say, “And who is my neighbor?” We knock down ten thousand men who were standing so that we may establish our own will, a thing deserving not merely to be left standing but to be buried. We transpose and transmute everything in order to give substance to what has no substance and form to what is formless. If once we let some word escape our lips, even if it later appears more ridiculous than ridiculous, we labor to prove it the voice of God, worthy perhaps for Moses and Aaron to hear—for it would be beneath us to say Ithamar and Eleazar, and still more beneath us to mention the multitude of Israel, even though they had first been purified for three days.

We do not perceive that our indecency is twofold: both in what we said badly at the beginning and in the battle we then wage to defend it. Blessed indeed is the man who has not sinned with his tongue, as someone has said; but praiseworthy too is the man who, after sinning, discovers his sin, recognizes it, and abhors it as something hideous—like a monster begotten in darkness, then seen in daylight, loathed, and cast away.

Shall we not imitate the humility of the Lord, who did not please himself but us, the many; who was bound as though he were a dangerous man in order to bind us to himself and to one another in the bond of peace; who emptied out his blood that he might gather into one the scattered children of God, so that there might be one flock formed from all the sheep under one Shepherd, escaping the leaderlessness before the wolves which rends them no less than the wolves themselves—and indeed gives the wolves additional strength to tear the flock apart?

## 17

What brought the woes upon the Pharisees? Was it not their love of honor and first place, and their desire to be called “Rabbi” by men? Did this very thing not make those miserable men killers of God?

I ask what the smoke of the Lord's wrath is, and Isaiah answers me: it is the proud man's “Do not touch me,” uttered by those whom the Lord opposes. Yet I can also show you a Pharisee worthy of imitation: Gamaliel, who proposed nothing Pharisaical against the apostles but, quite the contrary, gave reverent and God-fearing counsel on their behalf.

We are under necessity to exceed the Pharisees in righteousness, since otherwise we cannot attain the kingdom to which the One who justifies us has called and appointed us. Yet we surpass the Pharisees instead in our lofty opinion of ourselves. I do not know whether the result will be that we shut our brothers out of the Church or shut ourselves out of the kingdom.

Do you not see Paul choosing the men around Peter as judges of the Gospel he preached? Do you not see Peter rebuked by Paul and bearing the rebuke meekly? But as for you: unless you see everyone crouching beneath the thunder of your speech and falling face-down before its lightning—as the account of Tabor depicts the disciples falling before the radiance of the revealed divinity—you immediately dig up Simons and Marcions whom time has done well to bury and conceal. You stir the mud of the Gnostics, remodel the monstrosity of Sabellius, revive the madness of Arius and dark Photinus and the rest of the chain of sons of perdition—I refrain from naming them all lest even their names pollute the discourse—and you tie them to your brother as so many long ropes of blindness, though they contradict one another, so that when he tries to escape you may seize him, and not you alone but the others whom you summon as assistants under the pretext of zeal for piety.

Often these men do not even know what piety is. They merely long to acquire a reputation from condemning their brother.

Alas for the devices of the Evil One! Even now he makes idols into gods, inscribes vice with the name of virtue, and contrives that wickedness should be bowed down to and venerated.

## 18

But not so, servants of Christ, friends and brothers. Let us not estrange ourselves in this way from God, who draws everyone through kindness, while we through pride repel almost everyone. Let us not make episcopal thrones foundations for wickedness, nor turn the height of office into a tower of frivolity—for that is what I call opposition born from ambition and conceit.

Rather, to the degree that we are strong, let us bear the weak; to the degree that we are physicians, let us heal those who have been bruised. But if we ourselves grind them down—I am reluctant to say it, yet I shall say it—**that is the work of robbers, not physicians**.

I have said these things to you also against scoffers and despisers; concerning such men the Holy Spirit says that they shall accomplish nothing. But you, receive what is mine like a banker receiving a talent, and consider how you will return both it and its interest at the proper time. By “interest” I mean, according to my judgment, that you yourself discover in addition whatever the Spirit gives to the industrious seeker.

Strengthened by his grace, may we ourselves proclaim the Spirit purely, without adulteration, and according to the boundaries set by the Fathers, and teach others to do the same, in God the Father who teaches man knowledge and in Christ Jesus our Lord, to whom be glory unto the ages. Amen.

### Selected textual and interpretive notes

1. **Title and addressee.** The PG heading is badly corrupted in the OCR. The work is conventionally described as a discourse to a disciple or intimate concerning accusations against the Latins. The addressee is treated throughout as a younger ecclesiastic under Theophylact's spiritual direction.
2. **§1, “black-beaming Lucifer.”** The Greek *heōsphoron melanaugē* is intentionally paradoxical: a “morning star” that gives dark light. Theophylact is mocking polemicists who prove their alleged discernment by discovering ever more heresies in other Christians.
3. **§2, Sicilians.** The OCR reads *Sikaranous* in a damaged context. The sense is an illustrative regional abuse—people eating strangled animals—which Theophylact immediately distinguishes from official Roman ecclesiastical teaching.
4. **§3–6, procession and mission.** Theophylact's controlling distinction is between *ekporeusis*, which he reserves for the Spirit's hypostatic origin from the Father, and the Spirit's temporal or gracious *sending*, *bestowal*, *communication*, and *outpouring* through/from the Son. English “proceed” is therefore kept terminologically strict in these sections.
5. **§5, Latin linguistic poverty.** Theophylact's concession is significant. If Latin vocabulary forces one term to carry what Greek expresses by distinct terms, he is prepared to tolerate the wording in ordinary or even ecclesiastical speech provided the intended theological distinction is made clear. His refusal concerns its insertion into the Creed.
6. **§6.** The metaphysical reductio is rhetorically compressed and should not be treated as a polished scholastic syllogism. Theophylact's aim is to force a distinction between eternal origin and bestowal, not to construct a complete theory of causal ranking within the Trinity.
7. **§7, “skins.”** The tabernacle imagery contrasts more splendid offerings—gold, precious stones, purple—with the humbler animal skins Theophylact claims to contribute. He then Christianizes the image by making the skins “red” from the incarnate Word's blood.
8. **§8–9, unleavened bread.** Theophylact deliberately refuses an apologetic argument he considers historically false: he will not claim Christ avoided unleavened bread merely to secure a polemical advantage. His positive case is that the circumstances of the Last Supper do not create a perpetual liturgical obligation to reproduce every material circumstance of that meal.
9. **§9, “Sardinians.”** Theophylact invokes Western testimony against a universal Latin claim; the precise historical practice behind the allusion requires separate source-critical treatment.
10. **§10, Saturday fasting.** The argument invokes the Apostolic Canons, the Council in Trullo (the “Sixth” in Byzantine canonical usage), Laodicea, and Basil of Caesarea. Theophylact's appeal is historical-canonical: Western practice differs from a wider body of received custom, but he will soon insist that such differences are not necessarily church-dividing.
11. **§11, “stir up Camarina.”** This is a proverbial classical expression for meddling with a dangerous subject that is better left undisturbed.
12. **§12, physicians.** The strange reference to charms and miscellaneous amulets belongs to the analogy, not to a recommendation of magical medicine. His point is the hierarchy of medical aims: a physician may tolerate harm to a lesser part when saving a vital organ.
13. **§13, Peter and the keys.** The passage is sharply anti-papal in polemical force but not anti-Petrine: Theophylact's point is precisely that appealing to Peter while altering what he takes to be apostolic faith dishonors Peter rather than honors him.
14. **§14–15, *synkatabasis*.** “Condescension” translates *synkatabasis*: deliberate pastoral accommodation, modeled here on Paul's behavior toward Jewish Christians. It does not mean doctrinal compromise; Theophylact has just drawn the line at dogma.
15. **§15, the core ecclesiological maxim.** The Greek is unusually clear: *ou pan ethos aposchizein Ekklēsias ischyei, alla to pros diaphoran agon dogmatos*—“not every custom has power to divide Churches, but [only] that which leads to a difference of dogma.”
16. **§16–18.** The target of the closing invective shifts from Latin customs to Eastern zealotry. This is essential to the work's balance: after criticizing the *Filioque*, Theophylact spends his conclusion attacking the pride of those who manufacture heresies, refuse accommodation, and “tear apart the body of Christ.”

# IV. To the Porphyrogenitus Lord Constantine

*Conventionally called* Paideia Basilikē / Institutio regia. *Probable date: c. 1085–86. Greek base: PG 126, cols. 253–286; modern critical edition: Gautier 1980, pp. 178–211.*

 — Encomium

## 1. The worth and honor of this composition

I too, beloved emperor, give you this gift—a gift most fitting for me to give and for you gladly to receive. Perhaps it is even better than other gifts, which are consumed by time, exposed to envy, made easy prey for robbers and plunder for thieves. Your imperial subjects pay their yearly tribute: some gold, some silver, others things which, as the divine Apostle says, perish through use. But discourse is more precious than gold, brighter than silver, and, simply speaking, more honorable than them all.

Those others pay tribute even unwillingly — a very grave indictment of us. Wisdom, by contrast, is a possession unconfined and truly without a master: of its own accord it brings the fruit of its labor, while benefiting those who receive its gifts.

## 2. The duty of the emperor’s teacher

And so I, your teacher—for the very title gives me wings, and I grow ten cubits tall when I am called the instructor of an emperor—voluntarily bring you today this gift of discourse. I become a bearer of good news to you: do not distrust the voice of a teacher when I tell you that rivers of happiness will flow for you, if my words descend like rain upon your heart.

For this reason you have graciously accepted the freewill offerings of my mouth. I shall not flatter my emperor; I shall not pamper your ears with deceptive words that put the rhetorician on display. I shall not tune my music to the Lydian mode, but to the taut and Dorian one. For otherwise I should greatly wrong both the way of life I have chosen and your own nature—a nature certainly capable of yielding good and noble fruit, if only it receives royal seed.

## 3. Constantine’s good fortune in his native city

What is there that does not work together for you toward choosing the noblest life? Your native city is herself a queen: a great city and a beautiful city. Not even Momus himself could reproach her for failing to possess a climate properly tempered to the seasons or for lacking abundance and moderation in her air.

Winter here does not revolt against its limits and attack us in Scythian fashion, freezing the blood in living creatures and binding the rivers with chains of crystal. Nor does summer dissolve and cast down all bodily strength, rousing the dreadful Dog Star against the health of our bodies. Rather, winter is such as to brace our strength and gather the elements together, not allowing their array entirely to disperse and scatter; summer is such as to give warmth to the body and permit the exhalation of its excesses; and the remaining seasons are shaped proportionately in relation to their sisters.

Look also at the fruits of the trees in which we inhabitants of this fortunate city delight. They possess such size that, on seeing them, one hardly thinks to ask whether they are pleasant to taste; yet they delight the palate so greatly that, when tasted, their size is almost forgotten. Thus they surpass the large fruits of other places in sweetness, those distinguished for sweetness in extraordinary size, and those praised for both qualities simply by being offspring of the imperial city.

## 4. Constantine’s bodily gifts

Nature has also fashioned your body, giving it wings for every fitting activity and making it an unhindered servant of the operations of the soul. It is neither heavy, sluggish, and difficult to move, requiring many spurs, nor again more mobile than is proper, darting about without restraint. Escaping the excess of either extreme, it has been established within the bounds of the mean.

When it is necessary to mount a horse, to train yourself in repeated courses, to turn the spear this way and that with skillful movements and warlike exercises, your body is exceedingly light. So too in hunting: to ride ahead of the others, outstrip grown men, bring down a beast, and see it lying dead—another boy of your age could scarcely endure these exertions. Shooting from a horse flying at full gallop, and shooting accurately: all these are marks of agility.

Yet the decorum of your walk, the measured quality of your speech, and in general the thoughtful, almost philosophical character visible in everything—the fact that you do not wander about like one possessed by nymphs or whirl around irrationally—show that the mixture of the elements in you has been balanced, as though on scales, by the Higher Power.

## 5. His intellect and memory

And where, most receptive of pupils, shall we place your natural aptitude for learning, your quick understanding, and your memory? I am an unbribed and unimpeachable witness to them all; and with me bears witness this excellent company of men who have made trial of your nature.

Often some deed or discourse of the ancients worth reading is read aloud, and you, the pupil, anticipate me, the teacher, and seize the meaning before I explain it. And having seized it, you do not immediately lose it, but lay it up in the storehouses of the soul as a treasure that cannot be stolen.

Many learn quickly and forget still more quickly: as soon as they take something in, they cast it out again. It is like writing on water—the pen may move rapidly, but no trace follows the swiftness of its movements. In you nature has united qualities usually divided: speed of learning and incomparable memory. You are softer than any wax for receiving an impression, yet harder than any tablet for preserving it.

## 6. Constantine’s grandfather and father

Nor have the virtues of your ancestors escaped me, for through them also God has prepared you for every good thing. You, noble child, dearer to me than all emperors, have not received a nature such as my discourse has sketched only to be shamed by the baseness of your parents—as the proverb speaks of the squint-eyed, bright on one side and dark on the other.

No: your lineage on your father’s side is both royal and exceedingly just. In your blessed grandfather, rectitude and impartiality in judgment were more royal even than sovereignty itself. And what form of virtue did not shape the soul of your most godlike father? To whom else did he yield first place either in love of learning or in concern for divine things?

For him, indeed, **the imperial life was nothing other than books, association with learned men, and an eagerness to say something ancient and to hear something ancient.**

## 7. The nobility and piety of Constantine’s mother

As for your mother—who will give me the sweetness of Herodotus and the compact force of Aristides, so that I may omit nothing worthy of telling?

Your mother is royal by birth and the offspring of a most prosperous stock. She can count not merely illustrious fathers, grandfathers, and great-grandfathers, but myriads upon myriads of royal ancestors. Yet by her own deeds she has displayed a soul still more royal. To glorify her merely because of her imperial station would be like praising a pearl for its shell.

For what is extraordinary to her about empire? She counted all things as refuse, according to the Apostle, and chose to gain Christ alone. By him she was rescued even from the death of the flesh, having come to love the life hidden in him. So suddenly did she come to regard the height of royalty as a pit, and the humility that is in Christ as a truly heavenly height, that what is astonishing is not only the suddenness of the change but still more the ascent from one extreme to the other.

Many women indeed have passed into the solitary life, but often in a softer and more feminine manner. To become so fervently poor in spirit has been the particular zeal and discipline of the blessed Maria. Many others perhaps assumed the monastic habit under the force of countless necessities, driven unwillingly beneath the yoke by some goad. But what is unwilling is neither trustworthy nor lasting. In her, however, this above all is a cause for wonder: no calamity pursued her and no human being compelled her. Christ’s love alone drew her; she ran after the fragrance of his ointments and desired to sit beneath his shadow.

We admire even women swaddled from birth in poverty when they choose this hard and painful manner of life. How much greater wonder, then, do we owe our sovereign lady, who was born amid luxury and enjoyed a still more luxurious upbringing and manner of life, yet chose a life exceedingly bitter and laborious? She opposed rough garments to soft ones, black mourning dress to brilliant robes, little food and fasting to delicate tables. In short, because of the words of the Lord she kept to the hard road.

## 8. The almsgiving of Constantine’s mother

Perhaps someone has long been standing beside my discourse and blaming it because it has not yet described her philanthropy and mercy. My discourse wishes to do so, but loses courage before so vast a sea.

With what power of speech could one tell how many you have rescued from the noose of famine, how many naked bodies you have warmed, how many losses of children you have made lighter, how many you have comforted after the loss of fathers, how many women you have relieved after the deaths of husbands?

Nor, thrice-blessed lady, did you neglect Isaiah’s command to ransom those being led to death. You have turned the sword away from many necks; and when the darkness of death was already pouring over their eyes, you, a sun of mercy, scattered it.

## 9. Her secret gifts by night

Nor do your distributions by night remain hidden, though you have labored to conceal them. Even when performed in darkness, their light shines forth, and through them the heavenly Father is glorified.

Yet what your right hand does truly remains unknown to your left. For extending mercy is an action of the right hand; refusing to parade the good deed or sound a trumpet before one’s philanthropy means escaping the portion of vainglory that is sent away to Gehenna. This is how I understand the saying, “Let not your left hand know what your right hand is doing.”

## 10. Digression against ostentatious benefactors

And yet who does not know that our present age has introduced a novelty in a certain matter? I do not know exactly how it arose—and I would pray that the practice aims at a right purpose—but for many it has become a cause of scandal.

Ancient institutions devoted to the care of souls, worn down by time and needing a supporting hand, are passed by; while new and freshly founded establishments are raised under the names of their founders. Each man is eager to be both innovator and master of his innovation.

It is as though a man, while neglecting his own sick child whom he might heal, were to seek another wife so that he might father another child by her, though he had nothing to reproach in the one who was ill. Foolish man—indeed incurably sick yourself! You possess a good child and, when you could restore him, you leave him neglected, throw yourself into the cares of another marriage, and expend yourself on unnecessary labor.

## 11. The splendid benefactions of the Augusta Maria

But your mother, emperor, was not caught in such nets. Spurning human calculations and casting away to earth the desire to have her name invoked over lands and foundations, she made herself a temple of the kind the master-builder Paul describes. Christ was its foundation, and through the poor as her workmen she built upon it silver and precious stones.

She distributed much to those citizens most in need of mercy. She also brought comfort to many communities of monks and nuns—all of them fighting manfully against the ruler of this world. Nor did you allow the islands lying opposite us to remain outside your concern; there too you sowed your seed, which in its proper season you will reap with rejoicing and without weariness. Thus the islands themselves might say to you: “Rejoice with us, you who have just become our savior and nourisher.”

As for what you do for those who come to you day by day—how you receive each as an angel of God, as Christ Jesus; how your giving is done with cheerfulness and, together with cheerfulness, abundance—who could recount it all? Or rather, who would not become ridiculous by attempting to encompass everything?

A man could sooner count how many times in a day you breathe than how many times you show mercy. For with you, breathing and showing mercy are the same thing.

## 12. Her studies and great learning

And how great is her gentleness! Her love of learning exceeds every human expectation. What book, whether dense and difficult, has escaped her? What ancestral narrative?

At one time one may find her dwelling among the more perfect writings, taking doctrine as solid food; at another, lingering among simpler works as among the flowers of a delightful and graceful meadow. In brief, she meditates on the law of the Lord night and day. Rising and walking, sitting and resting, she pursues the divine sayings and, in threefold fashion, inscribes them upon the tablets of her heart, as David, Moses, and Solomon have declared.

## 13. Her admirable care for her son’s education

What remains? Childbearing is good, and the education of children is praiseworthy and pleasing to God. It is even sufficient for a mother’s salvation, if Paul is to be believed when he assures us that mothers will be saved through seeing to the self-control of their children.

Who, then, has conducted a child through the dangerous years of youth with such dignity? Who has stationed more instructors beside her son? Some ennoble his speech, others train his mind, others gather for him the lessons of history; all of them contribute something to the young man.

You blush at these praises, dove of the Lord, beautiful and complete—and for that very reason you are still more worthy of praise. The man who hunts after praises should not be praised, for he is vain; the one who shrinks from them deserves hymns all the more, for he is a philosopher.

## Part II — Counsel

## 1. Most wretched is the emperor who abuses gifts received from God

Do you hear, emperor? I have not sung all this without purpose, nor merely to chant empty songs, as the proverb has it. I have done it so that I may show you how many good things the Higher Power has bestowed upon you and how entirely possible it is for you to surpass all emperors who have ever lived, since you possess so many aids toward what is noble.

Who could be happier than you, if to all these gifts you add the crowning summit of your own virtue? And who could be more wretched, if by your later deeds you soil them all?

## 2. Imperial dignity is a disgrace to one who lacks imperial virtues

Do not imagine that the imperial office itself will add to your glory unless you also display a royal character. Otherwise the mad Cambyses and the effeminate Sardanapalus would be more illustrious than Aristides and Epaminondas.

Rather, the more conspicuous your station, the more conspicuously ridiculous you will make yourself if you disgrace it. If a private citizen lives irrationally and mocks the laws of virtue, even his neighbor may fail to know it. The sin of a private man does not naturally spread among many; it tends to be enclosed within his little household. It is not so with an emperor. Even if he has merely conceived the intention of doing wrong, he will not escape the notice of the multitude.

## 3. The emperor is praised for virtue, not clothing

Do not suppose that your gold-covered and purple-bordered robe will subdue men who serve Ares, men with a terrible and lion-like gaze, unless they also see you clad in bronze armor and personally engaged in war.

A barbarian is not struck with terror when he sees a man dressed like a bride. He laughs at one glittering with gold as at a little boy; and, mocking him as childish and unmanly, he thinks quite reasonably that he will not even need a fist to kill him.

## 4. The emperor must despise the love of pleasure

But if the enchantment of pleasures has mastered you—what a splendid emperor you are, and how worthy to rule everyone else!

If a man has made himself a triple slave to ignoble pleasures, how will he teach others not to rank law beneath pleasure? He has certainly educated himself wonderfully! How will he lead others toward what is better, when he himself refuses to listen to those who admonish him?

## 5. Love of toil is fitting and useful for an emperor

You must therefore banish and cut out by every device—by fire and iron—the most dreadful beast, pleasure. In its place you must introduce and array against it the labors that train emperors physically and make them formidable against their opponents.

These labors keep the flower of youth from withering in the young, and in those grown old they lighten the infirmity that comes with age, accompanying a man throughout his years like contemporaries and fellow-nurslings.

## 6. Three sound and three corrupt forms of government

Come now and learn, as briefly as possible, the pattern of kingship.

There are three principal forms of political constitution. One is monarchy which, when lawful, is called *basileia*, kingship — being, as the name itself suggests, the *basis* and support of the people. Another is composed of a number of rulers who themselves act lawfully; its name is aristocracy. The third is the participation of the whole people in the administration of the commonwealth, which they call democracy.

Opposed to these are three other forms, which do violence to what is proper. Tyranny lies in wait for kingship; oligarchy shoots at aristocracy, whenever certain wealthy and violent men subdue the best and rule over them; and facing democracy stands ochlocracy, mob-rule, a lawless and utterly disorderly gathering of a confused multitude.

I shall say nothing further about the others.

## 7. The first mark of a tyrant: he comes to power by violence

Hear instead the distinguishing marks of tyranny and kingship.

First, the tyrant forces his way into rule. He does not receive the reins of government from the citizens; he seizes them himself through slaughter and bloodshed. Such are the preludes of tyranny: from its very beginning it is sprinkled with blood.

## 8. The second mark of tyranny: inhuman assault upon the property and freedom of subjects

Once he has entered the palace, what rites does the tyrant perform? He champions every pleasure of the body and devises every sorrow for his subjects. He trusts no one, has no friend, and makes and considers everyone an enemy.

He hates the good because he believes himself hated by them—for good men naturally hate one unlike themselves. He hates the wicked precisely because they are wicked and desire the same things he does; he trembles lest someone more wicked than himself seize the government.

Thus he is always cutting down the tallest ears of grain. He reduces everyone to poverty and compels everyone to mourn and beat his breast. His reasoning is: let no one live at ease, lest leisure give him time for curiosity, curiosity give rise to thought, and thought to designs for change.

He alters laws and shifts them about, indulging either the greed of his soul, his licentiousness, or some other passion. He does not know how to maintain an army for the sake of the commonwealth. Instead he gathers bodyguards around himself, feeds and fattens them as guardians of his body, rearing the whole band against the commonwealth and giving them free rein to slaughter, treat men as nothing, and leave families childless.

## 9. The misery of a people subjected to tyranny

When a tyrant rules, even the farmer harvests tears. He is worn away by daily labor only to see his crop swept off by a tyrannical whirlwind; instead of grapes he cultivates the bramble of grief, whose thorns pierce his heart. Worst of all, he becomes prey both to enemies and to his own rulers.

The sailor curses the winds and the sea for not having drowned him, so bitter a sea and so much more tyrannical a storm has he experienced on land.

Those who are eminent in the state surpass every excess of misery, precisely because they dwell nearest the Minotaur and are most ready to be devoured. One man’s wealth is carried away. Another man’s daughter, formerly guarded in honor in her bridal chamber, is seized for violation. The father who had expected to raise songs and applause at the wedding now raises cries of grief, lamenting the outrage; in place of tears of joy he pours out tears of sorrow. Another sees his wife dragged to a hateful bed, as though a dart had been driven into his heart.

And if any of them so much as mutters a free word, the sword is immediately dipped in his entrails. This alone becomes sweet and welcome to those pitiable men: by the sword they are finally carried away from the sight of such things.

## 10. The tyrant’s anxious fears and perpetual torment

That beast, the tyrant, cannot even look upon his wife and dearest relations with pleasure. He suspects her too; lying beside her, he trembles, shuddering even at sleep lest she hand him over in his bed to death, the brother of sleep.

For everyone else a meal provides good cheer; at the table the tyrant is most afraid, lest poison have been mixed with the food, lest his cup become a cup of death. Nor can the wretch trust his bodyguards. How could he? He fears them most of all because they carry swords nearest to him. He is forever wondering whether they will use those swords not for him but against him. He arms them against everyone else, but against the bodyguards themselves he has no one whom he can arm.

Such terror is a fitting reward for a tyrannical and distrustful mind. No one can exact from a tyrant a greater penalty than the punishment he inflicts upon himself by placing himself under such necessities.

## 11. The entrance of the just king brings peace and joy to his subjects

You have seen the shape of the tyrant—how repulsive and accursed it is. Now look at the beauty of the king: how desirable, how lovable in itself; such as yours can be, if you choose.

In a way I have already painted kingship while painting the tyrant, since the true king will be his opposite. Still, I shall add a few more colors.

First observe how he paints the very entrance to his reign in colors opposite to the tyrant’s. He does not acquire rule through violence; he does not dye his imperial robe in blood. He receives it through the goodwill of the multitude and the sober, reasonable consent of the people. He receives kingship as the prize of virtue, all yielding what is greater to the man who is better.

So too among bees there is a natural king, and the entire swarm makes him its leader. Thus the forecourts of the true king’s reign are cheerful and approachable, not gloomy and difficult of access. Let us now proceed farther in and behold kingship itself.

## 12. The highest degree of piety is necessary and fruitful for a king

The true king knows that, just as the lower parts of a house or a ship must be most secure, so too kingship must stand upon a safe foundation. He therefore makes piety his foundation.

He is so devout that he scarcely yields first place even to priests, and he does and says everything with the knowledge that God sees all and hears all. Being a lover of God, he is shown to be beloved by God; for the man who loves God will be loved by God.

Thus God will be everything to him: father and brother, soldier and ally—granting success in war and raising trophies with him, and in peace working eagerly with him for good order. Who is happier than the man who has God for a friend? Who is more unconquerable in battle? What truce could be more peaceful?

Indeed, this man alone almost seems to rule the entire world. For if the world belongs to God, and he is acknowledged to be God’s friend, and friends hold their possessions in common, then the emperor who most loves God will in this sense be lord of the world.

## 13. Faithful friends are necessary instruments of good government

How, then, will the king supervise everything? How will he attend to and observe what is done in every place—praising one man, reproving another, correcting a third?

Do you wish to learn how all this may be done? Through the winged chariot of friends. The king himself is one man; through his friends he becomes many.

## 14. Friends are among the greatest goods of kingship

Since I have mentioned friends, I shall teach you something more fully about them. Among the many goods belonging to kingship, nothing is like the reality and the very name of a friend.

The tyrant surrounds himself with fear; threats and executions are his supposedly strong and impregnable defense. You, instead, build your wall out of friendship and fortify yourself above all with goodwill.

Nothing is so weak as a man hated by many, even if guarded by many spearmen. A citizen may bow down, magnify the ruler, acclaim him, and ask the Higher Power to add years to his life; but his tongue has sworn allegiance while his mind remains unsworn. His lips have become wet with praise while the roof of his mouth has not. He is afraid to say openly that he hates and abhors the ruler. But once he finds an opportunity, he reveals his hatred not in words but in deeds, and tastes the raw liver of the man he hates.

## 15. The service of a sincere friend to a king

A friend is not like this. Night and day he cares for the king who is his friend and is consumed with anxieties on his behalf. He does not look only to what he can gain or how he can harvest profit from office; he looks to how affairs may remain firmly settled for the kingdom.

He will cause a little pain in order to bring greater joy. He will be sharp in his words in order to make the outcome sweet. He will not bend himself to every wish of the emperor, behaving like a shadow that nods whenever the ruler nods and moves whenever he moves. He will be truthful and frank in judgment, making his friend’s concerns his own through love.

## 16. The king must avoid the harm caused by flatterers

The king, for his part, will not think his friend a burdensome disciplinarian but a welcome counselor. Toward flatterers, however, he will grow bitter as toward enemies and draw his brow severely against them.

For the servants of flattery truly mix a deadly honeyed drink. They kiss, but bite at the same time; they fawn, but tear; honey drips from them, yet it produces an aftertaste more bitter than gall.

To such men the king will open neither the doors of the palace nor those of his ears. He will sail past them as past the Sirens, stopping his hearing with wax and with wise words.

## 17. The king should obey and endure the admonitions of friends

To his friends, however, the king will submit himself even to cutting and cauterizing, as to skilled physicians. He will summon into the palace every man who is wiser than himself. He will draw many a worthy man out of an obscure corner instead of leaving such men to whisper their wisdom only among the young. Entrusting himself to them, he will make his own heart into a reservoir capable of receiving every excellent thing poured into it.

## 18. How the king should test good friends before entrusting them with government

These friends he will eventually place over the administration of cities—but only after making trial of them and learning that they will govern well.

What is this test? Follow my argument carefully and perhaps I shall hand you the touchstone by which gold is recognized. Three things must be considered in this trial: the man, the household, and the city.

First examine the man and see whether he has put his own character in order, so that neither anger nor desire—those servants and subjects of reason—has risen up and become master. When you find such a man, raise him to the administration of a household. If you find him approved there also, lift him to another step and entrust him with the government of a city.

For how can a man who does not govern himself properly preside over a household as he ought? And how can one who has not ordered a household govern a city?

Let this be your infallible rule for selecting officials: bring to public administration those who have been tested in more particular and private responsibilities. “He who is faithful in little is faithful also in much,” as we have learned. Likewise the Apostle does not entrust a Church to a man who does not know how to manage his own household.

## 19. The emperor’s favor powerfully draws the pursuits of his subjects after it

If your officials are men of this sort, they will quickly improve the empire and will produce many others like themselves. For whatever emperors devote themselves to has a natural tendency to increase among their subjects.

If the emperor loves rhetoric — good heavens, what a deluge of rhetoricians! The palace will be choked with readings. If he is thought to attend to philosophy and turn his mind toward the sciences, men full of Plato will stand at the imperial doors, and even Archimedes, Euclid, and Hypsicles will no longer be unknown to the gatekeepers. If he gains a reputation as a lover of horses, the imperial precincts will become cramped with Nisaean and Italian horses.

## 20. It is shameful and destructive for unjust men to prosper under an emperor

But suppose, on the contrary, that the ruler does not even know where on earth those who pursue virtue live and does not grant them so much as the sight of his face, while men who are conspicuous workshops of every kind of wickedness are rewarded by him with dignities and receptions. Suppose he sets such men over cities—merchants in unjust profits and collectors of filthy gains—and permits them to butcher the sheep, provided only that they bring the fattest portions back to him. Suppose he confounds every divine and human right so long as he can receive an obol from it.

I hesitate to say what such a ruler should be called. The Persians might call him a *huckster*, as they called Darius. Yet perhaps not even “huckster” is enough: ignoble though the word is, it is far too humane. Call him rather a robber, a blood-stained murderer, and—to put it plainly—a thrice-accursed wretch: a man who murders others merely to make a profit and manufactures afflictions for his subjects so that he himself may wallow without restraint in pleasures.

## 21. The ruler must attend to the commonwealth as a pilot attends to his ship

The emperor must not seek to take the greater share of pleasures. He must willingly place upon himself the greater weight of labor and care.

Do you not see the pilot of a ship? He is continually at work. While the others sleep pleasantly and pass over the deep in slumber, he stands guard in wakefulness. Even if by day he seizes an opportunity to sleep, his sleep is continually broken and turned back into waking: “Furl the sail! Let out the sheet! Turn the rudder! The yard is creaking! Look ahead—avoid the reef!” Thus even while sleeping he is in a sense more active than those who are awake.

So let the emperor give himself over to labor. Let him make the imperial office not a place of luxury but a place of care.

## 22. The emperor must direct wars and take part in them with judgment

Let him conduct his wars himself, present everywhere he can be and seeing everything. Yet he must not throw himself irrationally into dangers. He is not to die the death of an ordinary soldier, but to act as a calculating general who has raised many trophies of victory.

## 23. In peace the emperor should practice the exercises of war and honor veteran soldiers

While at peace, let him practice war. Let him continually train himself in every form of military exercise, refusing none of them, and let him train his soldiers together with himself.

He should also use as teachers men who are old and experienced in war, even those no longer able to fight. Gray hair has something wiser to tell the young.

An old man and a young man are each one half of a whole: the old man is the mind, the young man the hand, each needing the other. Nothing has harmed Roman affairs so greatly as the absence, at the head of affairs, of a strong old man whose outer shell has been wrinkled by time while the fruit within has ripened to perfection, like a walnut.

## 24. Bodily exercise is healthy for an emperor

Let the emperor make this a rule for himself: never to take food without first having sweated.

Through such exercise his body will become healthier and free of excess, and the rational part of him will not be hindered by being buried under a mass of bodily superfluities. What enemy could stand against such an emperor?

## 25. It is unworthy of an emperor to devote himself to actors and jesters; he should live with sensible men and converse with the educated

How could such a ruler have leisure for mimes and jesters? How could he fail, rather, to draw everyone toward goodwill when they see him caring and training on their behalf?

Consider the opposite. If the palace is opened to stage-performers and obscene men; if the imperial residence welcomes people worthy of being thrown into a pit so that they may behave shamefully in company with the lord of the inhabited world, exchange buffooneries with him, and share the imperial seat while degrading reason itself; while, on the other hand, men worthy even of ruling have the first gate slammed in their faces by the doorkeepers—what follows?

A man will receive honor because he imitated a parrot or twittered like a nightingale, while those who speak things worthy of human beings—or rather, utter things divine—will wander about dishonored. They themselves are nourished on almost nothing while fattening the lice upon their bodies. Insolence and shamelessness will have prizes set before them, while virtue will be left as a useless possession, full of dust and mildew.

How do you think affairs will go under such conditions? It is obvious to everyone even if I say nothing.

## 26. Humanity and clemency are proper to an emperor

How great should the emperor’s humanity be? As great, so far as humanly possible, as God’s own humanity toward mankind.

He will not make the sword his first resort, but will use it only when necessity compels him. He will imitate the King above. And if he will not be ashamed before the King, let him at least be ashamed before the bee, which nature was once thought to have made the one stingless ruler among the bees.

The man who readily draws the sword is not a king but an executioner. He does not possess God as his archetype and is not fashioned after him, but resembles avenging demons that feed upon human blood.

## 27. To what extent the emperor should use the sword and punishments

Yet I am not blunting the sword altogether. Let no one fasten upon my argument and tear my words to pieces.

I know, most excellent emperor, that your scarlet shoes and garments are symbols of fire. Fire has a double power: to give light and to burn — that is, to confer benefit and to punish.

Use punishments, then, but as sparingly as possible. **Give light more often; burn more slowly.**

The lamp carried before the emperor teaches the same lesson: it is there only to give light, not also to burn. **You too, then, shine by your good works.**

## 28. The emperor should surpass his subjects in virtue and wisdom

Above all, guard this principle for me: the man who is going to rule human beings must in virtue be more than ordinary human measure.

The ruler of sheep is not himself a sheep, but a human being. The ruler of cattle is not an ox but a rational overseer, far superior in nature to the creatures he leads. In the same way, the ruler of human beings must stand above those he governs in excellence, just as the shepherd stands above the sheep and the herdsman above the cattle.

## 29. Conclusion of the present work and promise of another like it

I shall not weigh you down by giving you too much advice at once. Perhaps I shall reserve something for another table, and, God granting it, prepare for you what remains—provided only that I see you receive these things with appetite, digest what you have eaten, and through them grow toward spiritual maturity.

## 30. God is promised as Constantine’s helper if he obeys this counsel and his mother’s commands

Words honor you; honor words in return. And remain forever obedient to your worthy mother.

If you submit yourself to her reins, you will always have God as a fellow-worker in your deeds and as a helper in your reign. Your mother’s virtue and blessing will surely obtain this for you: descending upon your head, it will strengthen both your imperial house and the house of your soul.

# Selected textual and translation notes

**Title and genre.** The convenient title *Paideia Basilikē* belongs to the early modern editorial history of the speech. Modern discussions commonly identify the manuscript work by its address to Constantine and classify it as a *basilikos logos*. The second half, however, is genuine *parainesis* — admonitory counsel — and can reasonably be read alongside Byzantine “mirrors for princes.”

**I.1 — “a very grave indictment of us.”** Greek: *tēs hēmeteras katakriseōs to barytaton*. The point is sharper than “grievous to our conscience”: subjects’ involuntary taxation constitutes an accusation against those who exact it.

**I.2 — “freewill offerings of my mouth.”** The phrasing is biblical in color and likely evokes the Septuagintal idiom of voluntary offerings. I have kept that sacrificial resonance rather than flattening it to “what I freely say.”

**I.3 — Momus.** The mythic personification of fault-finding. If even Momus cannot criticize Constantinople’s climate, no critic can.

**I.6 — Michael VII and books.** Theophylact’s compressed line is unusually vivid: for Constantine’s father, “the imperial life” consisted of books, learned company, and zeal to say and hear something ancient. I have preserved the repetition of “ancient,” which is part of the portrait rather than redundant English.

**I.7 — Maria “rescued even from the death of the flesh.”** Greek *anerrysthē* means “was drawn up/rescued.” The following phrase, “the life hidden in him,” strongly echoes Colossians 3:3. The sentence praises Maria’s ascetic conversion in deliberately paradoxical language: bodily mortification becomes rescue from bodily death.

**I.9 — right and left hand.** Theophylact playfully exegetes Matthew 6:3. The “left” becomes the vainglorious impulse excluded from the act of mercy.

**I.11 — building a temple through the poor.** The imagery combines Paul’s temple/building language in 1 Corinthians 3 with almsgiving. Christ is the foundation; the poor are, strikingly, Maria’s “workmen.”

**I.12 — “in threefold fashion.”** The Greek explicitly says *trissōs*. Theophylact then names David, Moses, and Solomon, weaving together scriptural motifs of meditation, repeated instruction, and inscription upon the heart.

**I.13 — “through seeing to the self-control of their children.”** The Greek is more specific than the earlier “conscientious upbringing”: *sōphrosynē* denotes self-control, chastity, or disciplined soundness of life. The passage adapts Pauline language about salvation “through childbearing” to Maria’s care for Constantine’s formation.

**II.6 — *basileia* as the “basis” of the people.** Theophylact offers an intentionally rhetorical etymology, linking *basileia* (“kingship”) with *basis* (“base/foundation”) and *laos* (“people”). It is not historical linguistics. The pun matters because it makes legitimate monarchy, by definition, the support of the people.

**II.7 — consent and tyranny.** The tyrant’s first mark is seizure of rule “through slaughters and blood,” rather than receiving the “reins of government” from the citizens. Theophylact is describing a moral typology of legitimate and illegitimate rule, not laying out a modern constitutional theory; nevertheless, the language of civic reception and consent is explicit.

**II.8 — “leave families childless.”** The OCR preserves *ateknoun*, “to make childless.” The earlier draft’s generic “commit outrage” softened a deliberately brutal climax.

**II.14 — the raw liver.** There is no need to domesticate the image. Greek: *tōn hēpatōn ōmōn apogeyetai* — the secretly hostile subject, once opportunity comes, “tastes the raw liver” of the hated ruler. The grotesque metaphor belongs to the rhetoric of concealed hatred.

**II.18 — man, household, city.** The three-stage test of an official moves from self-government to household government to civic government. Theophylact then explicitly invokes the maxim “faithful in little, faithful also in much” and Paul’s requirement that a church overseer be able to manage his household.

**II.19 — the emperor’s tastes reproduce themselves institutionally.** The exclamation *babai* has been allowed some force: “good heavens, what a deluge of rhetoricians!” The “sophists” here are professional rhetoricians/teachers, not necessarily fraudulent arguers in the modern sense.

**II.20 — Darius the “huckster.”** The insult draws on the classical tradition that Persians nicknamed Darius a retailer or huckster because of his fiscal exactions. Theophylact intensifies it: even that ignoble name is “too humane” for a ruler who profits from predatory officials.

**II.26 — *philanthrōpia*.** I translate the imperial virtue here as “humanity,” with “clemency” in the heading. In Byzantine political and theological diction *philanthrōpia* is richer than modern “philanthropy”: it means humane, merciful love for human beings and is especially associated with God’s disposition toward humanity.

**II.26 — the king bee.** Theophylact uses the ancient belief that the king/queen bee was stingless. The natural ruler of bees becomes a rebuke to a human ruler too eager to draw the sword.

**II.27 — the damaged ending.** The secure Greek reads: “Use punishments, then, but as sparingly as possible. Give light more often; burn more slowly. … You too, then, shine by your good works.” Immediately after that final clause the OCR degenerates into nonsense. I have not supplied a conjectural antithetical ending. A publication-grade edition should verify this locus directly in Gautier’s critical text and manuscripts.

**II.30 — “Words honor you; honor words in return.”** The Greek is deliberately lapidary: *Logoi timōsi se, tima kai sy tous logous.* “Words” here means cultivated discourse, learning, and the instruction the speech itself embodies.

# V. Oration to the Emperor Alexios Komnenos

## 1

The custom of our people, emperor, brings my discourse up into the palace—a custom royal in the truest sense, opening the imperial residence to the sophists of each generation. Yet even if no such custom existed, I would have brought you my acclamation of my own accord.

A man would have to be exceedingly crooked in judgment, left-handed in soul, and incapable of loving the beauty of noble deeds if, possessing a tongue and the power to speak, he silently passed by the flowering of your achievements. Indeed I should expose myself to two charges at once. I should wrong the blessings you have given us, through which I possess the freedom to speak openly; and I should slander my own nature by showing it invulnerable to so many good things and hostile to beauty.

Since by speaking we gain so much—giving justice her due and driving away an evil reputation—how could we not pray that strange Trojan prayer: that a voice might be given us in arms and hands, in hair and in the very tread of our feet, so that every member might contribute its share to the common fund of praise? For you have given every one of those members relief from the barbarians.

It seems, then, that just as we have profited from the excellence of your deeds, we are also to gain no small benefit from speaking about them; for we too shall be reckoned to possess some share in the good, because today we have consecrated our tongue to your praises.

But old Isocrates! You seem to have been distressed because you lacked the opportunity to go down into the theater and display the beauty of your voice before an assembled people. We have obtained what you longed for—but we do not possess what you possessed. Here is a contest of speeches, and a very great one; but the sophist falls far short of his reputation, especially when his power must be weighed against achievements of such magnitude. I do not lack material; what I lack is a fire great enough to kindle from that material a beacon visible through the air from afar.

I think, therefore, I shall pass over the rest. Many have already composed speeches upon those things, and I myself have adorned my tongue with them on another occasion. I shall mention only some part of the emperor's virtues, and by that small portion give an indication of the whole.

## 2

Those philosophers who investigated the nature of the virtues seem to me to have been deprived of a very great example. Had they been fortunate enough to live in our own time, they could have spoken with a witness before their eyes, looking toward the emperor. As it was—for they possessed nothing upon which to fix their gaze—they offered bare definitions without security in experience.

Our emperor, by contrast, displays alive and embodied in deeds what they deposited lifeless in letters. At the head of his rule he sets true and noble courage—not the bastard counterfeit that lies about the name, but the courage virtue recognizes and embraces as her own daughter: the courage that sets the beautiful itself before her and for its sake enters danger. A good father might perhaps display such courage for a noble child, and a noble child for his father.

You possess the reality of courage, emperor, and therefore have no need of the verbal divisions philosophers make concerning it. Permit me nevertheless this little display, and allow me to speak briefly about it; perhaps the digression will not be wholly without charm even for you.

There is, then, a true courage that does not disgrace its name: an unshaken condition of the heart before those things that human beings rightly fear and before the death that duty demands. Men who possessed it conquered not only enemies but time itself—the former immediately, the latter for as long as the human race endures.

All the other forms seem to possess only the most distant echo of this courage. Some are bold through experience, as sailors are before storms and surging seas. Some through fear of blows, like Xerxes' soldiers of old, driven toward the battle-line under the lash. Some through excess of anger, like boars rushing upon swords. Some through confidence born of previous success, like men who have often defeated their adversaries and therefore enter the next struggle with greater daring. Others through ignorance, deceived because they do not realize they are opposing men stronger than themselves—and as soon as they discover the truth they turn their backs.

All these forms dwell far away from courage proper.

You, however, neither need to be whipped forward into what must be dared—for rashness and panic lie far outside your practical judgment—nor do you give your back to those who remain in the field. Your chest is thick-set and your heart a workshop of fire. Yet you have enthroned reason as king and stationed anger beneath it as a spear-bearing guard, leaping only in whatever direction reason commands.

This courage of yours astonishes even the barbarian who holds Persia, whether at play or in earnest. At his drinking parties he even sets up mixing bowls to your health, embracing you with his ears and clasping you through report; for the sound of your nobility has gone out into all the earth. He has heard that you yielded nothing to the much-renowned Franks either in word or deed: your deeds overturned the expectations by which they were deceived into making war, and your words, marked by greatness of action and greatness of soul, compressed barbarian arrogance.

Those same qualities, noble emperor, made you formidable also to the Huns. Many cities—by your hands, I swear it—purchased your friendship and enrolled themselves as allies against the accursed Scythians, asking only that your unconquered right hand be raised threateningly over their enemies.

## 3

The Scythian did not wait for these developments to mature. Thinking more wisely than a barbarian might be expected to think, and conquering himself by the better counsel, he chose a road far more profitable than his former raids. He raised his right hand no longer with a sword but stretched it out for a handshake. He chose to worship the emperor unarmed whom he had been unable to endure armed; he preferred to possess as a peaceful master the man whom in battle he had discovered to be unconquerable.

And who does not know the strange way of the Scythians? Their onset is as swift as lightning, while their departure is at once heavily laden and astonishingly fast—heavy because of the multitude of plunder, fast because of their skill in flight. Whether coming or going, they outrun report itself; they do not even allow those who pursue them time to hear where they have gone.

More extraordinary still, they overrun everyone else's country while possessing no country properly their own. Let a man be bolder even than Darius son of Hystaspes, bridge the Danube, and go hunting Scythians: he will simply be pursuing the uncatchable in madness. They will wrap themselves in rocks and clothe themselves in thickets, while he will accomplish nothing more than wandering through mountains and forests—things only slightly less savage than the men he pursues—and become the spectator of that vast Scythian emptiness.

The proverb too understood this. If a pursuer tries to force the nature of the affair, he himself will perish, pitied less for his misfortune than hated for his rashness, while the Scythians will prove themselves indeed offspring of rocks and oaks: they will inflict suffering and suffer nothing. So, if we need not surrender everything to myth, I should say that Gyges himself, striking unseen while remaining unstruck, must have been a Scythian.

For these men a peaceful life is a calamity and quiet an illness. They imagine themselves to stand upon the summit of happiness whenever they possess either a plausible occasion for war or an opportunity to outrage a treaty. The most warlike among them is the man who proves most barbarous and most faithless, sacrificing with a maddened hand and enslaved to desperation.

Worst of all, in number they surpass the bees of spring. No one has ever seen their thousands or tens of thousands; their number is precisely their innumerability.

Yet against men so violent in onset and so countless in multitude, fear of you alone proved equivalent to ten thousand wars. It was enough to make them rest the horse, plant the little tent, and loosen the strap of the shield.

## 4

But see how the Scythian's trick almost escaped me. He was longing for peace and would gladly have sold even what was dearest to him to obtain it, yet he handled the business more cleverly. He dressed men up as ambassadors and sent them to the mighty sovereign, instructing them to say not that *they* needed peace but that they had come because *you* needed it, in order to lay down terms for you.

Now even ordinarily, in conversations with barbarians, the emperor hides philosophers and rhetors alike in his shadow: vigorous in thought, pure in expression, fertile in invention, effortless in utterance, large in conception and delightful in exposition. He fills conversation with greatness of mind and makes his words themselves royal and elevated.

But on that occasion, embittered as well by the barbarian's deception, he surpassed those Homeric speakers. Where a more violent charge was required, there was Odysseus; where the tension needed to be relaxed a little, there was Menelaus. At one moment his speech was concise and ringing; at another it fell thick as winter snow.

What happened then? Having incurred no small disgrace and learned that their desire to deceive had itself deceived them, the envoys split open their masks and exposed their faces. They confessed that they were thirsting for peace, having already felt your fire from a distance. Men who previously could scarcely settle anything except by blood now entrusted their affairs to writing and treaties.

Thus even a contentious barbarian yields to superiority in virtue, and royal godlikeness conquers a beast-like nature.

## 5

What a white and radiant day that was, when after so long—so very long—we erected a trophy of peace! What imperial hands, winning the victory before the war had even gone into action! What blessed times, in which **we gave peace rather than received it**.

If anyone longs to embrace a victory still fresh and unworn, let him fill himself with the emperor's achievements. Had you crowned the war with dangers, the toil of armies, and rivers of blood, the deed would still have been great and would have hammered many tongues into instruments of praise. But the marvel now surpasses even that: the enemy did not wait for your hand to strike. Condemning themselves beforehand, they rendered a just verdict against themselves. In this one respect they were no longer barbarians or Scythians: before suffering the injury they recognized the stronger man.

Another ruler might not even have received the embassy of such a nation. He would have shown himself harsher than the occasion required, raised his brow higher than the moment justified, and refused to end the punishment until he had feasted the beast of anger upon Scythian blood.

Your novelty lay precisely in the opposite. You did not trample upon men already fallen, nor reject suppliants. When you had seen them snorting with arrogance, displaying an iron sinew in the neck, you displayed in return your own unyielding strength. But once you found them contracted in fear and lowering their insolence, you pitied their fortune and praised in deed the maxim “Nothing in excess.” You judged that here the wise king's saying, “Do not be excessively righteous,” contained excellent philosophy. You did not inflict upon them everything they deserved to suffer; you displayed toward them what it was fitting for *you* to do.

For to gorge oneself upon punishment belongs neither to kingship nor to God. It is Erinys-like and demonic, allotted to evil natures and powers. Do you not hear the sacred sayings, that affliction is sent through evil angels? And does not God himself say that he does not will the death of the ungodly nor delight in the destruction of the living? David, Ezekiel, and Solomon bear witness to me.

Therefore you too, God's lieutenant and our emperor, did not pour your bile upon the Scythians. You laid your right hand upon them, granted treaties to those who sought them, and restored to the Roman Empire as to a mother many cities that had been carried away as captive daughters.

And now the farmer sleeps dreams of good omen because you keep watch on our behalf. No longer does he see in sleep one moment a pursuer, another a captor; one moment someone binding him, another bringing the sword down upon him. The sun rises and he goes out to his work until evening. The sun knows its setting; the farmer returns from his labor, spreads a table richly furnished by fearlessness, fills a free man's mixing bowl, rejoices in your sovereignty, belches contempt for the Scythians, plays with those dearest to him and embraces them gladly, boasting that because of the great Alexios he can see these things and be seen by them.

Why have I not yet spoken of those from the East who have come over to you, some individually and others together? Courage and gentleness dispute which of them brought these men, since each has converted them to your side. Whichever virtue is judged the victor, the emperor is the one who wins.

You have devised irresistible charms for us: you soften the implacable. And what is more divine, you act as God's matchmaker to them. You introduce God into their kingdom and make him a member of their own senate. Washing men of blood in the saving springs, you make them children of light; you clothe them in the garment of incorruption and refashion them with a gentle and saving formation, so that Christ may receive from you, as from Paul, the offering of the nations.

Look: our emperor has emerged as an apostle as well. This has risen from the argument almost as an unexpected corollary.

You may tell me the myth of Prometheus fashioning human beings. I shall set against your myth the truth of the emperor's *promētheia*—his providential foresight—refashioning barbarians into what is human, or rather into something more divine. You have even established among them a teacher of the nations in faith and truth, to cut straight for you, as Paul says, the furrow of the word and enlarge God's cultivated field.

## 6

Why speak of distant things? Why am I carried off toward the Danube and the East? Why do I not show that the emperor's philanthropy begins at his own hearth?

Not long ago a certain man arose, making himself out to be an ear of grain sprung from imperial seed—Paul would have called him an “operation of error.” He gathered around himself certain light-headed and counterfeit men, men deserving, according to Hesiod, to be called drones and, according to Archilochus, apes.

O height and magnitude of imperial majesty! O splendor of the palace and incomparable magnificence of the throne! What sort of creature dared insult all this, imagining the gifts of virtue something to be seized as plunder? Yet the foolish Adonijah proclaimed himself king together with his conspirators, the whole wicked faction.

And then? He who reveals deep things and interprets mysteries disclosed the evil bond to the emperor's ear and allowed the murderous design to be detected without effort.

What, then, do you behold here? The emperor's bitterness and inexorability? How did he avenge himself upon murderers who had not yet acted? How did he repay sword-bearers whose hands had not yet struck?

He stripped the hair from their heads, depriving them of their natural ornament because they had behaved shamefully toward the Head and insulted it. He stripped their beards as well, I think displaying by this symbolic appearance their unmanly and ignoble character. A stake was erected and the deceiver was bound to it; but the humane emperor, as it were, laughed at the stake itself. He sharpened it only into an object of fear, fashioning a mere bogey. For he would not permit the reign of Alexios to look upon the blood of a Roman man.

How many feats of arms is this one act worth? What equal to it can you find among the ancients?

A story is told of Constantine of all-famed memory. A certain insolent and arrogant crowd—and what is a crowd if not a heap of every kind of vice and an initiate in outrage?—pulled down his statues in the marketplace and disfigured their faces with stones. When the news reached the emperor, many around him behaved as though something terrible had been done, sharpening the arrow of imperial anger. Constantine calmly felt his own face with his hands and said, “Did they perhaps strike *this* with stones? Have they stamped any scar upon these cheeks?” When they replied that no mark of insult appeared upon the imperial face, he said, “Then let us leave the drunken fellows alone. They have done *us* no harm; they merely raged against bronze.”

That was a great thing, emperor, worthy of Constantine's soul—but it remained great only until you and your deed appeared.

When the lovely sun rose, the other stars disappeared.

Those men insulted shadows and had done no great damage to public affairs, so it was not so extraordinary that Constantine forgave their punishment. But this wicked faction all but brought the iron down upon your very neck. By forgiving *them*, you leave the ancients no room for comparison.

This is how you teach us to behave toward those who wrong us.

## 7

For even if in other matters you are more righteous than Solomon, here you willingly relax exact retribution. Distributive justice and corrective justice have been brought by you to extraordinary perfection; but justice according to strict retaliation is deliberately more moderate in your hands. You prefer to keep those who offended you indebted to you: men who owed much, received remission of much, and thereby learned to love justice in proportion to what was forgiven them.

You know, certainly, the Gospel verdict. A human being tends to harden himself against what resists him but yields to what softens him, becoming more pliable beneath persuasion as though beneath the healing oil of a medicine.

Will anyone say, then, that your gentleness grows from an excessively soft disposition, so that it should be called disease rather than excellence? May our own minds never become so diseased as to believe the form of royal virtue disfigured by such scars and rents.

Your character remains firmly established upon the majesty of rule while showing also sweetness and grace. The manliness is not harsh; the graciousness is not girlish. Dignity is not allowed to rear itself into arrogance, nor cheerfulness to collapse into cheapness.

Will you ever become soft, you man of adamant, you unyielding one? When you have leisure from the movements of war, you do not throw yourself into horse-races and the madness of the factions; you do not feminize your hearing with the clatter of unmanly music. Instead you awaken the sleeping force of spirit through hunting and the chase, giving your nobly born sons a taste of the feats proper to their age, as one trains young hounds.

As for temperance, I know you do not ask us to produce testimony. We see how you discipline the body by continual exposure in the open air and by the harsher exercises. From the beginning you loved one woman, the noble one among women, a consort worthy of empire. Appropriately, the shoots that spring from the root of this marriage are themselves of corresponding quality.

And now among these parents stands the beautiful, the universally desired one, the long-awaited sight—the young eaglet, already sharp of eye and only waiting for the swift wings to grow.

But why, father, have you become slower than nature itself toward your child? Do you not recognize your imperial son as an emperor? Why shrink back from the proclamation everyone desires? Why refuse to share the name with one who by nature already shares the reality? Even if you refuse, there remains an immovable law of nature: the cub of a lion is a lion.

## 8

Your prudence makes both foreign and domestic affairs its possession. Even when you win a war it claims a share in the victory, and it does not permit its sister Courage to complain that the inheritance of trophies is being divided with her. In the administration of cities, however, Prudence insists on presiding by herself.

For you have acquired, as a man truly possessed of understanding, precisely this art of steering your government. Prudence gives such commands as these: choose rulers who will care for their subjects as fathers care for children.

“Do you not see,” she says, “the great King who is your archetype, how he stationed one angel over one nation and another over another boundary, all of them turned toward him and working together for the good—none of them carving up the subject as meat for himself, fattening himself while making the citizens thin?”

Every sort of useless expense has been driven from the palace: buffoons maintained for the theater and men whose occupation is to be laughed at. You, emperor, drove the idle drones out of your hive, creatures who carry their stings only for evil, so that they should not siphon away the honey produced by those who labor.

You stand almost alone as teacher of mankind, educating them by deed that we were fashioned for good works. You have learned that idleness has taught every vice, and that as a rule men unwilling to mind their own work become exceedingly busy about the affairs of others.

## 9

We know how much self-restraint and order the emperor demands throughout the imperial household—almost as much as we demand of priests. And perhaps this too is in some sense a “royal priesthood.”

I have often wondered about this privately. Looking more intently into the matter, I even found myself at a loss, until at last I discovered the sovereign cause of such good order: your blessed root, your holy mother.

If the firstfruits are holy, surely the lump is holy as well. She is for you both supplier and procurer of every good. She gives you also your unity. You are branches, divided into distinct persons, yet made one in the root and drawing your quality from her.

See how the discourse itself, the moment it remembered her, united you! At first I enjoyed addressing the emperor alone. But once I remembered her, immediately I remembered also that greatest and most honorable head to me—the first after the first, the mightiest emperor after the mighty emperor.

Her golden tears give us a victory without tears. Her all-night groanings give us the shouts of joy and songs of triumph. She, emperor, crowned you with the trophy over the Scythians.

Shall I sing to you from the Song of Songs, since this is an occasion of victory and festival? “Go out and see him in the crown with which his mother crowned him on the day of the gladness of his heart.”

For this reason, even when you are not physically present, you are present in public affairs: she assumes the cares of everyone as no one else assumes the care of one. And when you *are* present, in another sense you are absent, entrusting the whole to her greatness of mind.

“Many women have done mightily, but you have surpassed and risen above them all.” Solomon joins us in ministering the encomium. Ah, how far you have advanced beyond even his prudence!

David too sings of you as a mother rejoicing over children—not simply children, blessed woman, but add children's children, and perhaps even their descendants, to display your happiness more clearly.

O woman rich in every blessing, a sight that cannot be exhausted! Wondrous in yourself, yet more wondrous through those who come from you. They have set themselves before all humanity as a law embodied in action, proclaiming brotherly love through themselves and chastening our life with eloquent silence.

For formerly imperial power was something that could not be shared, or could scarcely be shared. Hence the proverb about Thyestean banquets, and not even the long passage of time has been able to draw a curtain over that polluted table. But these men have shared power with gladness: the two care for one thing, and the one thing cares for the two. I do not know how else to express it.

It therefore seems to me that God, wishing to show a second and newer creation of political affairs, placed in the heaven of empire **the two great lights**. This, surely, is the wise saying of Ecclesiastes: “Two are better than one.”

O men who alone have repaid one another with equals: alone conquering everyone, yet alone conquered by one another!

Blessed are we who are ruled by you, and more blessed than those who came before us: more blessed than those ruled by one because we are ruled by two, and more blessed than those ruled by two because we are ruled by *such* two.

Those among us who stand near you are more blessed still, because they stamp your characters upon their souls and retain the impressions more deeply than signet-rings retain their engravings. Does not this circle surrounding you incline toward the center of your virtue and from it receive straight and equal dispositions? All have reached the summit of wisdom and dignity and are filled with justice.

## 10

And should I not prophesy that such an empire will provide us even with “birds' milk”—everything that could possibly be desired? If anyone calls me a seer, I shall not reject the name. I would even call myself, without blushing, a reader of omens: I look toward you, the eagle appearing on the favorable side, and from you receive the sign of prosperity for the inhabited world—truer than Melampus, more exact than Polydamas, more pious than Balaam.

All will therefore enjoy the common goods in common. [And learning too must share in them,] for it is in danger not merely of falling to its knees but of falling flat upon its face. I will not accept that my emperor should appear less devoted to letters than Hadrians, Marks, and Commoduses, men who maintained sophistic eloquence at public expense. I will not accept that, when Themis sits in judgment, you should fail to be judged superior in this respect also to the emperors before you.

They restored a custom that time had allowed to disappear and returned public oratory to the palace. You must build upon their foundation something more: restore **eagerness** in speaking as well.

As for me, children, I have laid down the warp for this robe of imperial praise. You now throw in the woof. I have handed you the craft, so that the robe may become more variegated and more complete.

### Selected textual and historical notes

1. **Genre.** This is an imperial *basilikos logos*, a panegyric delivered for Alexios I Komnenos. Theophylact organizes much of the speech around the canonical virtues, especially courage, gentleness, temperance, and prudence.
2. **§1, the “Trojan prayer.”** The allusion is obscure in the damaged PG text. The sense is secure: Theophylact wishes every bodily member could become an organ of speech so that the whole body might praise the emperor who has freed it from barbarian danger.
3. **§2–5, archaizing ethnonyms.** “Persians,” “Franks,” “Huns,” and especially “Scythians” are rhetorical/classicizing labels, not precise modern ethnic terminology. The Scythians in the central peace narrative are associated in modern scholarship with the Pecheneg threat; the other labels likewise require event-by-event historical identification rather than mechanical ethnic substitution.
4. **§2, courage.** The classification of counterfeit forms of courage is drawn from the classical philosophical tradition. Theophylact's distinctive move is to identify Alexios as the living instance that earlier philosophers lacked.
5. **§3, Gyges.** The invisible Gyges belongs to the classical philosophical/mythic tradition. The joke is that Scythian hit-and-run warfare makes an unseen attacker seem naturally “Scythian.”
6. **§4, Homeric speakers.** Theophylact contrasts styles associated with Homeric orators, especially Odysseus' verbal force and Menelaus' concise restraint; the allusion to words like winter snow evokes the famous description of Odysseus' speech in *Iliad* 3.
7. **§5, “we gave peace rather than received it.”** The distinction is central to the panegyric: Alexios' peace is represented not as capitulation but as a victory so complete that the enemy seeks terms before being struck.
8. **§5, conversion.** Theophylact deliberately recasts imperial diplomacy as apostolic work. The emperor's incorporation and baptism of eastern barbarians makes Alexios, rhetorically, a second Paul and refashions classical Prometheus imagery into Christian imperial “providence” (*promētheia*).
9. **§6, conspiracy.** The pretender is likened to Adonijah. Theophylact stresses Alexios' refusal to shed Roman blood: public humiliation is represented as severe enough to terrify but restrained short of execution.
10. **§6, Constantine.** The anecdote about Constantine feeling his face after a crowd mutilated his statues is an exemplum of imperial clemency. Theophylact's competitive claim is that Alexios surpassed it because his conspirators threatened the living emperor rather than bronze images.
11. **§7, the “young eaglet.”** The passage concerns the imperial child and presses Alexios toward public recognition of the son's imperial status. The image depends on dynastic continuity: a lion's cub is already a lion by nature.
12. **§9, the holy mother and the “two great lights.”** The mother is Anna Dalassene; the second male ruler praised alongside Alexios is his brother Isaac Komnenos, whose exceptional position under Alexios makes sense of the language of paired rule. The panegyric deliberately presents concord between imperial brothers as the opposite of the murderous rivalries proverbial in monarchy.
13. **§10, “birds' milk.”** A proverbial impossibility standing for the rarest imaginable luxury: under such rulers, Theophylact jokes, even the impossible might be expected.
14. **§10, final appeal to students.** The closing address turns from emperor to the rhetorician's pupils. Theophylact presents his speech as the warp of a woven garment and invites them to provide the woof—an unusually explicit glimpse of imperial panegyric as pedagogical performance.
